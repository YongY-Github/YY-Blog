AI Reasoning Evolution: From Strategic Games to LLMs

1. The Foundations of Mechanised Thought

In computer science, reasoning is typically defined as the capacity for discrete thoughts to build upon one another in order to generate new conclusions — specifically conclusions structured so that an external observer can follow the logic and reproduce the same result.

Historically, one of the benchmark environments for testing this capability was the “blocks world” problem, where an artificial agent had to manipulate objects into a desired configuration.

These early experiments revealed an important threshold:

Once solutions exceeded roughly eight reasoning steps, many early AI systems deteriorated into what researchers described as “reasonable-looking gibberish.”

The systems could imitate logical structure for short chains of thought, but lacked the flexible reasoning required for longer planning horizons.

A modern example of this rigidity appears when Large Language Models (LLMs) are instructed to play tic-tac-toe while intentionally trying to lose. Despite the simplicity of the game, many models fail because they remain trapped in strongly learned patterns (playing to win). This highlights an important insight:

True reasoning requires more than sophisticated pattern mimicry.

The Two Pillars of Reasoning

Functional reasoning systems generally require two architectural components.

Pillar Definition Chess Example
World Model An internal simulator or representation that predicts environmental changes resulting from specific actions. The rules of chess and the current spatial arrangement of pieces.
Algorithm A decision-making procedure that evaluates and selects among possible actions using the world model. The logic used to determine whether a move improves or worsens the board position.

Historically, AI systems approached this through two major search paradigms.

The Greedy Approach

The system evaluates only the immediate next move and selects the option with the highest short-run value.

For example: - capturing a piece, - gaining material, - or maximizing immediate reward.

This is computationally efficient, but strategically shallow.

Exhaustive Search

At the opposite extreme, the system attempts to evaluate every possible future path.

This quickly becomes computationally impossible.

The chess game tree contains more possible board configurations than there are atoms in the observable universe.

2. The Rise of Machine Intuition

A major transition in AI occurred when researchers shifted away from manually coded heuristics toward neural networks capable of learning environmental intuition through experience.

One landmark example was TD-Gammon in 1989.

TD-Gammon: - used a neural network to evaluate Backgammon positions, - trained through millions of games of self-play, - and improved using reinforcement signals derived from wins and losses.

Remarkably, it achieved near world-class performance using only a shallow one-step look-ahead.

Position Intuition vs. Move Intuition

Over time, researchers distinguished between two forms of “intuition.”

Position Intuition

The ability to evaluate the quality of a static state.

For example: - estimating the probability of winning from a given chess position.

Move Intuition (Policy Functions)

The ability to predict which moves are promising before explicitly calculating their consequences.

This resembles human instinct: - “This move feels strong.”

Human-Like Move Prediction

In 2014, Clark and Stokeley trained networks directly on human move patterns.

Their architecture: - produced probability distributions over legal moves, - ranked candidate actions according to human tendencies, - and learned stylistic preferences from expert play.

However, these systems plateaued at roughly amateur strength because they lacked a deeper search procedure to validate their intuitive guesses.

The AlphaGo Breakthrough

The decisive breakthrough came from integrating neural networks with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS).

AlphaGo combined: - policy networks (move intuition), - value networks (position evaluation), - and guided search.

Instead of exhaustively searching the full game tree, the system performed selective simulations (“rollouts”) focused on the most promising continuations.

This allowed computational resources to be concentrated where uncertainty was greatest.

Move 37

The defining symbolic moment came during Game 2 against Lee Sedol.

AlphaGo played the now-famous Move 37 — a move so statistically improbable by human standards that commentators initially assumed it was a mistake.

Instead, it proved brilliant.

The moment became historically significant because it suggested something deeper:

Machines were no longer merely imitating human strategies.
They were discovering new ones.

AlphaGo Zero and the Rejection of Human Priors

Later systems such as AlphaGo Zero removed human training data entirely.

The AI learned solely through self-play.

An important conclusion emerged:

Human examples acted as a “crutch” that constrained exploration.

Pure self-play allowed the system to discover strategic patterns beyond accumulated human tradition.

3. Generalised Learning and the “Dreaming” Concept

In 2018, Ha and Schmidhuber introduced the influential paper World Models.

The central idea was radical:

An AI could learn an internal simulator of reality and train inside imagined environments.

The system: - observed the world, - compressed experience into a latent representation, - and then generated simulated futures (“dreams”).

Training no longer required constant interaction with the real environment.

This dramatically reduced the cost of learning.

MuZero: Learning Without Rules

DeepMind later extended this idea with MuZero.

Unlike earlier systems, MuZero was not given: - the rules of chess, - the rules of Go, - or the rules of Atari games.

Instead, it inferred useful environmental structure directly from reward signals.

MuZero mastered: - Chess, - Go, - Shogi, - and 57 Atari games, without explicit knowledge of the governing rules.

The Transfer Learning Problem

Despite these achievements, a major limitation remained.

Learning one environment did not generalize to another.

A system trained for: - chess, could not automatically transfer its knowledge to: - driving, - robotics, - or language.

Each domain still required retraining from scratch.

This created isolated “skill silos.”

4. The Transformer Paradigm Shift

A profound change occurred in 2017 with the paper:

Attention Is All You Need

Originally developed for language translation, the Transformer architecture replaced sequential word processing with a mechanism called attention.

Rather than translating word-by-word, the system learned relationships between words through high-dimensional mathematical associations.

This enabled: - context sensitivity, - long-range dependency tracking, - and scalable representation learning.

Mechanics of Large Language Models

Tokens

Words are broken into numerical units called tokens.

These may represent: - entire words, - partial words, - or punctuation fragments.

Vectors

Each token is represented as a high-dimensional vector encoding: - semantic meaning, - associations, - grammar, - and contextual relationships.

GPT-2 used vector dimensions around 768.

Modern systems such as GPT-4 operate at vastly larger scales, though exact specifications are proprietary.

Pre-training

LLMs learn through exposure to enormous text corpora.

During training, the model continuously predicts the next token.

For example:

“The dog was barking…”

causes the system to increase associations between: - dog, - barking, - pets, - noise, - and related concepts.

Over billions of examples, the model develops a statistical representation of language.

5. Simulated Creativity and Prediction at Scale

Modern generative AI is fundamentally:

Prediction at scale.

When prompted with:

“The sky is…”

the model estimates probability distributions over possible continuations.

For example:

  • Blue → 47%
  • Clear → 18%
  • Falling → 12%

The system selects from these distributions.

Simulated Creativity

Creativity emerges when the system intentionally avoids the most probable continuation.

Instead of selecting: - the top prediction,

it samples: - the second, - third, - or lower-ranked possibilities.

This creates outputs that appear: - varied, - surprising, - and human-like.

Beyond Text

Importantly, the same mathematical principle extends beyond language.

Generative AI can predict: - the next pixel in an image, - the next frame in a video, - or the next segment of audio.

The underlying mechanism remains statistical continuation prediction.

Hardware Acceleration

This capability became practical largely because of Nvidia GPUs.

Originally designed for: - graphics rendering, - and later cryptocurrency mining,

GPUs proved exceptionally efficient at large-scale matrix multiplication.

This allowed modern data centers to compute billions of token relationships in real time.

6. Advanced Reasoning Architectures

Recent AI research increasingly resembles the search procedures of earlier game-playing systems.

Chain of Thought (CoT)

Prompts such as:

“Let’s think step by step.”

encourage the model to generate explicit intermediate reasoning stages.

This: - decomposes complex tasks, - reduces logical jumps, - and improves performance on reasoning benchmarks.

Tree of Thought (ToT)

Tree of Thought extends this further.

Instead of pursuing a single reasoning chain, the system: - explores multiple candidate reasoning branches simultaneously, - evaluates their plausibility, - and prunes unpromising paths.

Conceptually, this resembles Monte Carlo Tree Search applied to language.

Step-by-Step Verification

OpenAI’s paper:

Let’s Verify Step by Step

introduced reinforcement learning methods that reward valid intermediate reasoning steps.

Rather than evaluating only final answers, the system receives feedback throughout the reasoning process.

This resembles human social learning: - nods of understanding, - expressions of confusion, - approval, - or correction.

Over time, the model learns to: - suppress faulty logic, - and reinforce coherent reasoning patterns.

7. The Intelligence Debate and the Future of Reasoning

One major finding of recent research is the relationship between:

thinking time and reasoning quality.

Allowing models to generate more intermediate reasoning tokens often improves accuracy more effectively than simply increasing parameter counts.

The ARC Test

To move beyond memorization, researchers increasingly use the ARC Test (Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus).

ARC problems require systems to infer: - novel visual patterns, - abstract transformations, - and first-principles rules,

without relying on previously seen internet data.

The benchmark is designed specifically to test genuine generalization.

The Ongoing Philosophical Debate

The field remains divided between two broad perspectives.

The Plato’s Cave Argument

This view holds that modern AI systems are fundamentally: - advanced pattern matchers, - sophisticated mimics, - and statistical illusion machines.

According to this perspective: - reasoning chains are not “understanding,” - but only the appearance of understanding.

The Functionalist Argument

The opposing position argues:

If a system consistently produces valid reasoning and correct conclusions, the internal mechanism may not matter.

From this perspective: - sufficiently advanced mimicry becomes functionally indistinguishable from understanding.

And if it is indistinguishable in practice:

perhaps it is understanding.

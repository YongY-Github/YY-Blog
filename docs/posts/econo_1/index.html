Confounders, Mediators, Moderators, and Instrumental Variables

Many empirical problems involve a “third variable.”

For example:

  • omitted variables,
  • mediators,
  • moderators,
  • instrumental variables,

all introduce additional variables into an analysis. Yet these variables play fundamentally different causal roles.

One source of confusion is that regression coefficients may change whenever additional variables are introduced into a model. However, coefficient changes alone do not tell us why they changed.

This note clarifies the distinction between:

  • regression algebra,
  • causal interpretation,
  • and causal structure.

The key insight is:

regression algebra explains how coefficients mechanically change, while causal inference explains what those changes actually mean.

1. Omitted Variable Bias as Regression Algebra

Suppose the true model is:

\[ Y = \beta X + \gamma Z + u \]

where:

  • \(X\): education
  • \(Y\): wage
  • \(Z\): ability or ambition

Suppose we omit \(Z\) and estimate:

\[ Y = \alpha + \tilde{\beta}X + e \]

Then:

\[ \tilde{\beta} = \beta + \gamma \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Z)} {\text{Var}(X)} \]

This is the classic omitted variable bias formula.

What the Formula Says

Bias occurs whenever BOTH conditions hold:

  1. \(Z\) affects \(Y\):

\[ \gamma \neq 0 \]

  1. \(Z\) is correlated with \(X\):

\[ \text{Cov}(X,Z) \neq 0 \]

If either condition fails, omitted variable bias disappears.

A Crucial Observation

Notice something important:

the formula itself says nothing about causal direction.

The formula only depends on:

  • covariance,
  • and regression relationships.

At this purely statistical level, regression algebra only “sees” correlation.

It does not know whether:

\[ Z \to X \]

or:

\[ X \to Z \]

or whether a meaningful causal interpretation exists at all.

NoteTechnical Insight

The omitted variable bias formula is fundamentally a regression algebra result.

It explains when coefficients mechanically change after omitting a correlated variable.

By itself, however, the formula does not determine whether the omitted variable represents:

  • confounding,
  • mediation,
  • selection,
  • or some other causal structure.

2. Why Causal Direction Matters

The same statistical correlation pattern can arise from very different causal systems.

This is why causal direction matters.

Suppose we observe:

\[ \text{Cov}(X,Z) \neq 0 \]

and

\[ \text{Cov}(Y,Z) \neq 0 \]

This alone does not tell us whether \(Z\) is a confounder, mediator, or something else.

The causal role depends on the underlying structure.

3. Confounders

The standard confounding structure is:

\[ \begin{array}{c} Z \\ \swarrow \quad \searrow \\ X \rightarrow Y \end{array} \]

Here \(Z\) is a common cause of both \(X\) and \(Y\).

Example:

  • \(X\): education
  • \(Y\): wage
  • \(Z\): ability

Ability affects:

  • educational attainment,
  • and wages independently.

If ability is omitted, part of its effect is incorrectly attributed to education.

This is true confounding.

Intuition

A confounder acts like a contaminant.

It creates spurious association between \(X\) and \(Y\).

The goal is therefore:

to block the confounding pathway and recover the causal effect.

Direction of Bias

The sign of omitted variable bias depends on:

\[ \text{Bias} = \gamma \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Z)} {\text{Var}(X)} \]

For example:

Cov(\(X,Z\)) \(\gamma\) Bias
positive positive upward bias
positive negative downward bias
negative positive downward bias
negative negative upward bias

Thus omitted variables may:

  • exaggerate effects,
  • attenuate effects,
  • or even reverse coefficient signs.

4. Mediators (Intermediate Variables)

Now consider a different structure:

\[ X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y \]

where \(M\) is a mediator.

Example:

\[ Education \rightarrow Occupation \rightarrow Wage \]

Occupation is correlated with both education and wages.

However, occupation is not a contaminating variable.

Instead, it is part of the causal mechanism itself.

Education affects wages partly by changing occupational opportunities.

Mediation Is About Mechanisms

The key question changes from:

“What biases the coefficient?”

to:

“Through what channel does the effect operate?”

Mediation analysis therefore decomposes effects into:

\[ \text{Total Effect} = \text{Direct Effect} + \text{Indirect Effect} \]

where:

  • direct effect:

\[ X \rightarrow Y \]

  • indirect effect:

\[ X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y \]

Why Mediators Should Not Be Treated Like Ordinary Controls

Suppose we estimate:

\[ Wage = \alpha + \beta Education + u \]

Then \(\beta\) captures the total effect of education on wages.

Now suppose we include occupation:

\[ Wage = \alpha + \beta Education + \gamma Occupation + u \]

Now the pathway:

\[ Education \rightarrow Occupation \rightarrow Wage \]

is partially blocked.

As a result:

  • \(\beta\) no longer measures the total effect,
  • it now measures only the direct effect.

Thus:

controlling for a mediator changes the meaning of the coefficient.

This is fundamentally different from controlling for a confounder.

A Simple Mediation Decomposition

Suppose:

\[ M = aX + v \]

and:

\[ Y = c'X + bM + e \]

Substituting the first equation into the second:

\[ Y = c'X + b(aX + v) + e \]

which gives:

\[ Y = (c' + ab)X + (bv + e) \]

Thus:

\[ \text{Total Effect} = c' + ab \]

where:

  • \(c'\): direct effect
  • \(ab\): indirect effect through the mediator
NoteTechnical Insight

In mediation analysis, coefficient changes are not necessarily interpreted as “bias.”

Instead, they may reflect decomposition of the causal effect into:

  • direct pathways,
  • and indirect pathways.

The Sobel Test

The classical Sobel test examines whether:

\[ ab \neq 0 \]

That is:

Is the indirect effect statistically significant?

Modern mediation analysis often prefers:

  • bootstrap confidence intervals,
  • structural equation modeling (SEM),
  • or causal mediation frameworks,

because products of coefficients are often non-normal.

5. Moderators and Interaction Terms

Moderators are different from mediators.

A moderator changes the strength of an effect.

Suppose:

\[ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \beta_2 Z + \beta_3(XZ) + u \]

The interaction term:

\[ X \times Z \]

asks:

Does the effect of \(X\) depend on \(Z\)?

Example:

  • \(X\): education
  • \(Y\): wage
  • \(Z\): urban location

Then:

\[ Education \times Urban \]

asks whether returns to education differ between cities and rural areas.

Marginal Effect Interpretation

Taking the derivative with respect to \(X\):

\[ \frac{\partial Y}{\partial X} = \beta_1 + \beta_3 Z \]

This means:

the effect of \(X\) changes depending on the value of \(Z\).

Intuition

A moderator acts like a “volume knob.”

It amplifies or weakens the effect.

NoteTechnical Insight

Interaction terms do NOT measure indirect effects.

They measure changing slopes or heterogeneous effects.

Mediation asks:

Through what mechanism?

Moderation asks:

Under what conditions?

6. Instrumental Variables (IV)

Suppose ordinary regression is biased because:

\[ \text{Cov}(X,u) \neq 0 \]

This may occur because of:

  • omitted variables,
  • reverse causality,
  • measurement error,
  • or selection bias.

Instrumental variables attempt to isolate exogenous variation in \(X\).

Suppose:

  • \(W\): distance to college

Causal structure:

\[ W \rightarrow X \rightarrow Y \]

with the key assumption:

\[ W \not\rightarrow Y \]

except through \(X\).

The Logic of IV

A valid instrument must satisfy two conditions.

Relevance

The instrument must affect the endogenous variable:

\[ \text{Cov}(W,X) \neq 0 \]

Exogeneity

The instrument must be unrelated to the error term:

\[ \text{Cov}(W,u) = 0 \]

Why IV Works

Suppose:

\[ Y = \beta X + u \]

The IV estimator can be written as:

\[ \beta_{IV} = \frac{\text{Cov}(W,Y)} {\text{Cov}(W,X)} \]

Using the structural equation:

\[ \text{Cov}(W,Y) = \beta \text{Cov}(W,X) + \text{Cov}(W,u) \]

If the instrument is exogenous:

\[ \text{Cov}(W,u)=0 \]

then:

\[ \beta_{IV} = \beta \]

NoteTechnical Insight

IV estimation isolates the component of variation in \(X\) that is unrelated to the regression error term.

Conceptually, the instrument acts like a quasi-natural experiment.

Final Intuition

A useful way to remember the distinctions is:

Concept Intuition
Confounder contaminant
Mediator transmission belt
Moderator volume knob
Instrument natural experiment

The same variable may even play different roles depending on the causal structure and research question.

Thus causal inference is not merely about “adding controls.”

It is about understanding:

  • the data-generating process,
  • the causal structure,
  • and the meaning of statistical relationships.

Indonesia Is Still Growing. So Why Are Investors Losing Confidence?

Growth, Institutions, and the Politics of Economic Credibility

Indonesia
Southeast Asia
economic development
political economy
Author

-YY-

Published

October 4, 2026

Indonesia presents an interesting puzzle.

On the surface, the economy still looks relatively strong.

Growth has remained close to 5 percent. The country has more than 280 million people, enormous natural resources, a large domestic market, and an increasingly important position in global supply chains, particularly in nickel and electric-vehicle production.

Yet the mood surrounding the economy has become much less reassuring.

Credit-rating agencies have become more cautious. The stock market has fallen sharply. The rupiah has weakened. Foreign investors have become more nervous. Questions have also emerged about the credibility of some official statistics and the transparency of key institutions.

So how can an economy still growing at around 5 percent generate so much anxiety?

That is the paradox.

And I do not think the answer is that Indonesia has suddenly stopped developing.

The deeper issue may be that investors and households are beginning to ask a different question:

What kind of growth is Indonesia producing, and how durable are the institutions supporting it?

But before getting to the current policy debate, there is an even deeper problem.

Indonesia is extraordinarily difficult to govern in the first place.

Before the 5 Percent Paradox, There Is a Geography Paradox

Indonesia stretches roughly 5,000 kilometers from east to west.

It consists of more than 17,000 islands.

Most of those islands are small and lightly populated. Around 93 percent of Indonesians live on only five large islands: Java, Sumatra, Borneo, Sulawesi, and New Guinea.

But even that understates the imbalance.

More than half of Indonesia’s population lives on Java.

Java contains only a small share of the country’s land area, yet it is home to well over 150 million people.

At the same time, much of the country’s mineral wealth lies elsewhere.

Nickel.

Coal.

Copper.

Gold.

Large land areas.

Much of this is found on islands outside Java.

So Indonesia begins with a fundamental mismatch:

\[ \text{people and political power} \neq \text{land and natural resources}. \]

This is not merely an interesting fact about geography.

It shapes the entire development problem.

Factories want to be near workers and markets.

Workers want to be near jobs.

Governments want to place infrastructure where large numbers of people can use it.

But natural resources may be thousands of kilometers away.

The result is a country where economic concentration and physical resources are distributed very unevenly.

The Cost of Being an Archipelago

A normal country can build one integrated electricity grid.

Indonesia often has to build many.

A normal country can connect industrial centers by road or rail.

Indonesia repeatedly reaches the sea.

A normal country can move trucks across provincial borders.

Indonesia often needs ships, ferries, or aircraft.

That raises the cost of almost everything.

Electricity networks have to be duplicated.

Transport systems remain fragmented.

Shipping between Indonesian islands can be surprisingly expensive.

The attached narrative gives a striking example: a container traveling from Jakarta to Singapore could historically cost far less than moving a similar distance between Jakarta and another Indonesian city.

The point is not the exact price today.

The point is structural.

Domestic integration can be more difficult than international trade.

This can be summarized as:

\[ \text{distance} + \text{fragmentation} + \text{small local markets} \rightarrow \text{high logistics costs}. \]

Those costs matter because development depends on moving goods, workers, energy, information, and capital efficiently.

When internal transport is expensive, firms cluster even more strongly in already-developed regions.

That makes regional inequality harder to reverse.

Why Subsidizing Ships Is Not Enough

Indonesia has tried to reduce these disadvantages.

One initiative is the subsidized maritime network often described as the “sea toll.”

The government supports fixed shipping routes linking more remote islands to the rest of the country.

That helps.

Goods arrive more regularly.

Some prices fall.

But the attached narrative highlights a revealing asymmetry.

Ships may leave Java full of manufactured goods and consumer products.

Many return much emptier.

Why?

Because the eastern islands often do not produce enough marketable output to fill the ships on the return journey.

That creates a development problem that infrastructure alone cannot solve.

A shipping subsidy can reduce the cost of moving goods.

It cannot automatically create factories, firms, skilled workers, suppliers, or consumer demand.

In other words:

\[ \text{connectivity} \neq \text{productive capacity}. \]

The state can build the bridge.

It cannot instantly create the economy on the other side.

Why Everyone Lives on Java

At first glance, Java’s extraordinary population density looks irrational.

The island has dozens of active volcanoes.

Earthquakes are common.

Millions of people live close to volcanic hazards.

Jakarta itself faces flooding and severe land subsidence.

Why would so many people remain concentrated there?

The answer is that the same geology that makes Java dangerous also helped make it productive.

Volcanic activity repeatedly renews the soil.

For an agricultural society, that matters enormously.

Much tropical soil becomes nutrient-poor over time because heavy rainfall leaches minerals away.

Volcanic ash does the opposite.

It adds fresh minerals.

Java’s soils could support intensive agriculture and very high population density.

So the historical logic was not:

people foolishly settled beside volcanoes.

It was closer to:

\[ \text{volcanic activity} \rightarrow \text{fertile soil} \rightarrow \text{high agricultural productivity} \rightarrow \text{population concentration}. \]

The danger and the advantage came from the same source.

That is one reason Java became the demographic and political core of the archipelago.

Colonial Geography and Economic Path Dependence

Indonesia did not begin as a single unified state designed around economic balance.

Before colonial rule, the archipelago contained many kingdoms, sultanates, trading centers, and regional societies.

The Dutch East India Company did not set out to build a well-balanced modern nation.

It was a commercial enterprise.

It expanded where trade and resources justified expansion.

It controlled islands because they produced spices, commodities, or strategic advantages.

The Dutch colonial state later inherited and expanded that system.

When Indonesia became independent in 1945, it inherited the borders and much of the spatial structure of that colonial economy.

The political center remained on Java.

The capital remained in Jakarta, formerly Batavia.

Resources remained scattered across distant islands.

The country therefore inherited something close to the geography of a commercial empire.

That does not mean modern Indonesia is simply a colonial creation.

Quite the opposite.

The remarkable story is how much institution-building Indonesians had to do after independence to make such a fragmented territory governable.

Jakarta: The Cost of Concentration

Jakarta became one of the largest urban areas in the world.

That concentration created enormous economic advantages.

Firms could find workers.

Workers could find employers.

Banks, government agencies, universities, suppliers, consumers, and infrastructure clustered together.

Economists call this agglomeration.

The basic mechanism is self-reinforcing:

\[ \text{firms} \rightarrow \text{workers} \rightarrow \text{suppliers} \rightarrow \text{markets} \rightarrow \text{more firms}. \]

But agglomeration also creates congestion.

Land becomes expensive.

Traffic worsens.

Infrastructure comes under pressure.

Housing becomes more difficult.

And in Jakarta’s case, groundwater extraction contributed to severe land subsidence.

Much of the city was built on soft alluvial soil.

Where piped water did not reach households and businesses, wells became essential.

Groundwater was pumped out.

The soil compacted.

The city sank.

In some northern districts, the land now sits below sea level.

The important point is that Jakarta’s problem is not simply “rising seas.”

The city itself has been sinking.

That is the physical expression of overconcentration.

Nusantara: Can a Government Move Economic Gravity?

In 2019, Indonesia announced a radical response.

Build a new capital.

The new city, Nusantara, would be located on Borneo.

The idea is understandable.

Move political power closer to the geographic center of the country.

Reduce the burden on Jakarta.

Signal that Indonesia is more than Java.

Create a new growth pole.

Encourage development outside the traditional core.

But Nusantara also illustrates how difficult it is to alter economic geography.

Governments can relocate ministries.

They can build roads, airports, offices, and housing.

But they cannot instantly reproduce decades of agglomeration.

Jakarta has firms.

Universities.

Financial institutions.

International connections.

Suppliers.

Skilled workers.

Professional networks.

Customers.

Government offices are only one part of that ecosystem.

This gives us an important distinction:

\[ \text{moving the capital} \neq \text{moving the economy}. \]

Nusantara may succeed as an administrative center.

But even if millions eventually live there, Java will remain overwhelmingly important.

The project is therefore best understood as a symbol of Indonesia’s deeper structural problem:

how do you rebalance a country when economic gravity has been accumulating in one place for centuries?

The Achievement Is That Indonesia Holds Together

The geography is so difficult that it is easy to become deterministic.

One might look at the map and conclude that Indonesia should be unstable.

But that misses one of the most important facts about the country.

Indonesia has held together remarkably well.

That did not happen automatically.

It required institutional choices.

One was language.

Rather than adopting Javanese, the language of the largest ethnic group, the independence movement chose what became Bahasa Indonesia, derived from Malay.

That mattered.

A national language associated with the dominant population might have reinforced perceptions of Javanese control.

A language that belonged to no majority group could function as a more neutral national medium.

Another important choice was constitutional compromise.

Indonesia became the world’s largest Muslim-majority country while retaining a formally pluralist national framework.

That mattered for the Christian-majority areas of the east and for the broader challenge of maintaining unity across different religious communities.

Then came decentralization.

After the fall of Suharto, significant authority and resources were pushed down toward local governments.

This was politically risky.

But it also reduced the need for every regional conflict to be solved from Jakarta.

Indonesia became more decentralized without disintegrating.

And perhaps most strikingly, it built one of the world’s largest democratic systems across thousands of islands.

Ballot boxes travel by boat, helicopter, road, and on foot.

Hundreds of millions of citizens participate.

The point is not that Indonesian institutions are perfect.

They are not.

The point is that difficult geography did not mechanically determine failure.

Institutions mattered.

That lesson becomes very important when we turn to today’s economic debate.

The Memory of 1997

Indonesia’s current economic model cannot really be understood without the Asian Financial Crisis.

In 1997, Indonesia entered one of the most severe economic crises in its modern history.

Only a short time earlier, growth had been rapid.

But beneath the surface, vulnerabilities had accumulated.

Banks and corporations had borrowed heavily in foreign currency. Much of that borrowing was poorly hedged. Financial-sector debt had grown rapidly. When confidence in Asian markets collapsed and capital began leaving the region, Indonesia was hit particularly hard.

The rupiah depreciated dramatically.

Financial institutions came under severe pressure.

The real economy contracted by roughly 13 percent in 1998.

The crisis quickly became political.

Rising prices, unemployment, and social unrest intensified pressure on President Suharto, who resigned in May 1998 after more than three decades in power.

The crisis was therefore not simply a recession.

It was a collapse in both the economy and confidence in the institutions managing it.

And that experience shaped what came next.

Rebuilding Credibility

After 1998, Indonesia rebuilt much of its macroeconomic framework around the idea that credibility had to be protected.

Bank Indonesia became more independent.

Fiscal policy became more constrained.

A ceiling was introduced limiting the budget deficit to 3 percent of GDP and public debt to 60 percent of GDP.

These rules were not magical.

There is nothing economically sacred about the number 3.

A deficit of 2.9 percent is not automatically responsible while 3.1 percent is reckless.

But the rule served another purpose.

It acted as a commitment.

Indonesia was effectively saying:

We will not allow fiscal policy to drift indefinitely simply because short-term political pressures make more spending attractive.

The same logic applied to central-bank independence.

The point was not that central bankers would always be right.

It was that monetary policy should not become an easy instrument of day-to-day political pressure.

In shorthand:

\[ \text{credibility} = \text{rules} + \text{institutional independence} + \text{predictability}. \]

Over the following two decades, this framework delivered something valuable.

Indonesia grew at around 5 percent per year with remarkable consistency.

The pandemic was a major interruption, but the broader story was one of macroeconomic stability.

That stability mattered because Indonesia had learned, painfully, what happened when confidence disappeared.

The Next Ambition: Move Up the Value Chain

Stability, however, was not enough.

Indonesia did not want to remain simply a large consumer market and exporter of raw materials.

Nickel became the clearest example of a more ambitious development strategy.

Indonesia holds some of the world’s largest nickel reserves. Nickel is important in many electric-vehicle batteries and other industrial applications.

For years, Indonesia exported large quantities of raw nickel ore for processing abroad.

The government wanted more of that value to remain at home.

So Indonesia moved toward a policy of downstreaming.

In 2020, exports of unprocessed nickel ore were banned.

The message to foreign companies was straightforward:

If you want Indonesia’s nickel, process more of it in Indonesia.

This policy changed incentives.

Foreign companies invested in smelters and processing facilities.

Indonesia moved from being mainly a supplier of raw ore toward becoming a much more important center of nickel processing.

Billions of dollars flowed into the country.

From one perspective, this was a clear success.

The country used control over a strategic natural resource to force more industrial activity to occur domestically.

That sounds very much like successful industrial policy.

But the story becomes more complicated when we ask who benefited.

Geography Returns Through Industrial Policy

This is where the new geography story strengthens the old economic one.

Many natural resources are outside Java.

Many consumers, workers, financial institutions, and political institutions remain concentrated on Java.

That means industrial policy must do more than attract capital.

It has to build productive ecosystems in places where economic density may be much lower.

A smelter can be constructed relatively quickly.

A complete industrial cluster cannot.

It needs:

  • skilled labor;
  • reliable power;
  • logistics;
  • local suppliers;
  • housing;
  • schools;
  • healthcare;
  • finance;
  • transport;
  • institutions.

So downstreaming is partly a spatial-development strategy.

It asks whether resource-rich regions can become more than extraction zones.

Can they become centers of production?

Can economic activity move closer to natural resources rather than simply shipping those resources toward Java or abroad?

That is a much harder question than simply counting investment dollars.

Growth Is Not the Same as Broad-Based Development

Indonesia’s GDP continued to grow.

Investment increased.

The nickel industry expanded.

But many households did not seem to experience the same improvement.

One of the clearest warning signs was the middle class.

Indonesia’s middle class numbered roughly 57 million people in 2019, but had fallen to around 48 million five years later.

That is a significant reversal.

It matters especially because household consumption accounts for more than half of Indonesia’s GDP.

A country of Indonesia’s size cannot rely indefinitely on investment and exports while large numbers of households feel economically insecure.

The labor market helps explain the problem.

Almost 60 percent of Indonesian workers remain in the informal sector.

Informal employment often means lower and less stable income, limited social protection, and weaker career progression.

Even more educated workers have not necessarily been insulated.

Between 2018 and 2025, the share of highly educated workers in formal jobs fell.

So Indonesia may be creating jobs.

But it is not always creating the kinds of jobs that generate a secure and expanding middle class.

That distinction is crucial.

We can write:

\[ \text{GDP growth} \neq \text{broad-based improvement in household welfare}. \]

The two can move together.

But they do not have to.

The Nickel Paradox

The tension is especially visible in manufacturing.

Indonesia has attracted large amounts of Chinese investment into nickel processing and related industries.

At the same time, some traditional manufacturing sectors have come under pressure from cheaper Chinese imports.

Textiles, footwear, and electronics have faced factory closures and job losses.

This produces an unusual development paradox.

Chinese capital can simultaneously:

  • help build advanced new industrial capacity;
  • deepen Indonesia’s role in global commodity and EV supply chains;
  • and intensify competitive pressure on older labor-intensive industries.

The result can look impressive in aggregate statistics while feeling far less impressive to workers in affected industries.

This raises an important question about the structure of growth.

Suppose one new industry attracts billions of dollars in capital but employs relatively few workers.

At the same time, several older industries employing many more workers contract.

GDP may still rise.

Investment may still look strong.

Exports may still increase.

But the employment effects can be much less favorable.

This is the difference between:

\[ \text{quantity of investment} \]

and

\[ \text{quality and distribution of development}. \]

Indonesia’s challenge is not simply to attract more capital.

It is to turn that capital into productivity gains, better jobs, stronger domestic supply chains, and rising household incomes.

The Politics of 5 Percent Growth

This helps explain the political appeal of President Prabowo Subianto’s economic agenda.

If the old model delivered stability but large numbers of households felt that the benefits were not reaching them, then a promise to change the model becomes understandable.

Prabowo promised growth of up to 8 percent.

More importantly, he promised that ordinary Indonesians would feel more of the gains.

The response was a larger role for the state.

The government introduced a large free-meal program.

It announced ambitious plans involving housing, infrastructure, industry, and natural resources.

The objectives are not difficult to understand.

Better nutrition, better housing, better infrastructure, and faster industrialization are all defensible goals.

And in a country with Indonesia’s geography, the case for an active state is particularly strong.

Markets alone will not necessarily build uneconomic transport links to remote islands.

They may not coordinate new industrial clusters.

They may not equalize access to public services across a vast archipelago.

They may not solve Java’s overconcentration.

So the real question is not whether the state should be active.

It already has to be.

The question is:

Can Indonesia have a stronger developmental state without weakening the institutional constraints that made macroeconomic stability possible?

That is where the current uncertainty begins.

An Ambitious State with a Small Revenue Base

Indonesia’s government collects relatively little revenue compared with many countries at a similar level of development.

The government wants to undertake increasingly ambitious programs.

But the tax base remains relatively narrow.

In very simple terms:

\[ \text{ambitious spending} + \text{low revenue} = \text{financing pressure}. \]

That pressure can be resolved in several ways.

Taxes can rise.

Other spending can be cut.

The deficit can increase.

State-owned enterprises can be mobilized.

Capital regulations can be used to keep more resources inside the domestic system.

Public assets can be consolidated and invested more aggressively.

None of these options is necessarily wrong.

But each changes the relationship between the state and the economy.

And each can affect investor confidence.

Keeping Export Dollars at Home

One example is the government’s decision to require commodity exporters to retain more of their foreign-currency earnings inside Indonesia.

The logic is straightforward.

Indonesia earns large amounts of foreign currency from nickel, coal, palm oil, and other commodities.

If more of those dollars remain inside the domestic financial system, the supply of foreign currency increases.

That can support the rupiah.

In simplified form:

\[ \text{more export dollars retained domestically} \rightarrow \text{greater FX supply} \rightarrow \text{less pressure on the rupiah}. \]

But what stabilizes the currency from one perspective can constrain firms from another.

Exporters may need access to foreign currency for working capital, imports, debt service, and overseas investment.

So the same policy can be interpreted in two very different ways.

One interpretation is:

Indonesia is sensibly using its commodity strength to stabilize its financial system.

Another is:

the government is becoming increasingly interventionist in the movement of private capital.

The economic effect depends partly on implementation.

The political effect depends partly on trust.

Danantara and the Question of State Capitalism

Another important development is Danantara, the state investment fund created in 2025.

It took over management of a very large pool of state-owned enterprise assets.

The ambition is substantial.

The model is often compared with Singapore’s Temasek: use state-owned capital professionally to support long-term national development.

In principle, there is nothing inherently problematic about this.

A well-run sovereign investment vehicle can help coordinate investment, improve the management of state assets, and support strategic development.

And again, Indonesia’s geography gives coordination unusual importance.

Large infrastructure projects, energy systems, industrial zones, and regional development often require a scale of coordination that fragmented private investment may not provide.

But the size and political importance of Danantara create another question.

Will investment decisions be made primarily on economic grounds?

Or will short-term political objectives influence them?

That distinction matters because public investment funds depend heavily on governance.

The issue is not whether the state owns assets.

The issue is whether the institutions controlling those assets are:

  • transparent;
  • professionally managed;
  • accountable;
  • insulated from arbitrary political intervention.

Once again, the core issue becomes credibility.

Central-Bank Independence and the Memory of the Crisis

Perhaps the most sensitive issue is Bank Indonesia.

After the Asian Financial Crisis, central-bank independence became one of the symbols of Indonesia’s institutional rebuilding.

The logic was clear.

Monetary policy should not be casually subordinated to fiscal or political objectives.

Under the current government, Bank Indonesia has been working more closely with the government on financing and policy coordination.

Closer coordination does not automatically mean that central-bank independence has disappeared.

In periods of crisis or large structural transition, coordination between fiscal and monetary authorities can be useful.

But investors pay close attention to these changes because they remember what the institutional separation was designed to prevent.

Again, one appointment or one agreement does not prove institutional capture.

But markets often respond not to a single event but to a pattern.

And patterns matter.

The 3 Percent Rule

The debate over Indonesia’s fiscal rule may be the clearest symbol of the broader shift.

For more than two decades, the 3 percent deficit ceiling represented fiscal restraint.

The economic question is not whether 3 percent is the perfect number.

It almost certainly is not.

The institutional question is different:

What happens when a rule designed as a long-term commitment begins to look negotiable?

This is a classic commitment problem.

Suppose a government creates a rule today:

\[ D_t \leq 0.03Y_t. \]

Investors believe the rule constrains future governments.

That expectation lowers perceived fiscal risk.

But if the rule is repeatedly reconsidered whenever it becomes inconvenient, the value of the commitment falls.

The rule can remain formally intact while its credibility weakens.

That is why markets sometimes react strongly even before policy actually changes.

They are not merely pricing today’s deficit.

They are pricing the expected future rules of the game.

Why 5.12 Percent Became Controversial

This brings us back to the puzzle of official growth.

For the second quarter of 2025, Indonesia reported GDP growth of 5.12 percent.

That was stronger than economists had expected.

Normally, an upside surprise should be good news.

But this particular number generated unusual scrutiny because several other indicators appeared much weaker.

Vehicle sales were soft.

Layoffs were rising.

Credit growth was slowing.

Consumer confidence had weakened.

Manufacturing surveys looked less reassuring.

Yet official data showed strong growth.

The important point is not that the GDP numbers were necessarily false.

The available material does not establish that.

There can be perfectly legitimate reasons why GDP estimates diverge from selected high-frequency indicators.

The more interesting issue is what happens when institutional confidence has already started to weaken.

A surprising statistic is then interpreted differently.

Under high trust:

“Interesting. The economy performed better than expected.”

Under low trust:

“Why does the official number look so different from everything else?”

This gives us a useful relationship:

\[ \text{same statistic} + \text{different level of trust} \rightarrow \text{different interpretation}. \]

Credibility is therefore not just about whether a number is technically correct.

It is about whether people believe the process that produced it.

Markets Look Forward, GDP Looks Back

This helps explain the apparent contradiction between strong GDP growth and falling asset prices.

GDP is primarily a measure of recent production.

Financial markets are forward-looking.

An investor buying Indonesian assets today is not mainly asking:

What was GDP growth last quarter?

The investor is asking:

What will Indonesia’s returns and risks look like over the next five, ten, or twenty years?

A simple way to think about an asset price is:

\[ P_t = E_t(\text{future returns}) - E_t(\text{future risks}). \]

This is not a literal pricing equation.

It is an intuition.

If investors become more worried about:

  • policy unpredictability;
  • fiscal pressure;
  • central-bank independence;
  • transparency;
  • corruption;
  • capital controls;
  • institutional checks and balances;

then expected risk rises.

Asset prices can fall even while current GDP remains strong.

That is not necessarily a contradiction.

It is a difference in time horizon.

The economy can be growing today while investors become less confident about tomorrow.

Geography Makes the State Necessary; Credibility Makes the State Affordable

This is where the geography story and the institutional story come together.

Indonesia’s physical structure creates a strong case for state capacity.

Someone has to build roads, ports, electricity networks, schools, hospitals, sea links, and communications infrastructure across thousands of islands.

Someone has to address the enormous imbalance between Java and the outer islands.

Someone has to coordinate industrial development in resource-rich but less densely populated regions.

Someone has to manage disaster risk.

Someone has to maintain national cohesion across a huge and diverse territory.

So Indonesia cannot simply choose between:

\[ \text{state} \quad \text{or} \quad \text{market}. \]

It needs both.

But an ambitious state also needs financing.

And financing becomes cheaper when institutions are credible.

That creates a deeper relationship:

\[ \text{state capacity} \rightarrow \text{development} \]

but also:

\[ \text{institutional credibility} \rightarrow \text{lower risk} \rightarrow \text{cheaper capital} \rightarrow \text{greater state capacity}. \]

The two reinforce one another.

A developmental state without credibility can become expensive.

A credible state without developmental ambition can leave structural problems unresolved.

Indonesia needs both.

Growth Versus Credibility

This, I think, is the heart of Indonesia’s current economic story.

Indonesia is not suddenly poor.

It has not stopped growing.

It still has enormous advantages:

  • a very large domestic market;
  • a young population;
  • major commodity resources;
  • strong strategic importance;
  • expanding industrial capacity;
  • and substantial foreign investment.

The problem is subtler.

The old model emphasized macroeconomic discipline, predictable rules, and institutional restraint.

The new model wants faster transformation, broader redistribution, more industrial policy, and a more active state.

That tension can be written as:

\[ \text{stability and credibility} \quad \text{vs.} \quad \text{distribution and faster development}. \]

But perhaps that framing is still too simple.

The real challenge is to achieve both.

Indonesia needs a state capable of investing, coordinating, and supporting development.

But it also needs institutions strong enough to reassure investors that state power will be used predictably and transparently.

Development requires capacity.

Credibility requires constraint.

The difficult part is combining them.

The Constraint May Be the Asset

Governments often experience fiscal rules, independent institutions, and procedural constraints as obstacles.

They slow decisions.

They limit discretion.

They prevent governments from immediately doing everything they would like to do.

But that is precisely why they can be valuable.

A government that says:

“Trust us. We will behave responsibly.”

is making a promise.

A government that creates institutions preventing itself from behaving irresponsibly is making a stronger promise.

This is the logic of tying one’s own hands.

In institutional economics, credibility can arise because future behavior is constrained.

So the 3 percent fiscal ceiling, central-bank independence, and transparent investment governance should not be viewed only as restrictions on policy.

They are also assets.

They reduce uncertainty.

And uncertainty has a price.

The Political Economy Behind the Shift

There is another side to this story that should not be ignored.

If Indonesia’s existing economic model had been delivering rapidly rising prosperity to the majority of households, there might have been much less political pressure to change it.

The demand for a more active state did not emerge from nowhere.

It emerged partly because respectable headline growth did not always translate into secure formal employment or upward mobility.

That creates a political problem.

Macroeconomic stability can look very successful from the perspective of:

  • bond markets;
  • credit-rating agencies;
  • foreign investors;
  • central banks.

But voters experience the economy differently.

They ask:

Is my income rising?

Can my children obtain good jobs?

Can I afford housing?

Do I feel more secure than five years ago?

Is development improving my life?

These are not irrational questions.

So Indonesia faces two constituencies whose concerns are both legitimate.

Markets want:

\[ \text{predictability} + \text{discipline} + \text{institutional independence}. \]

Households want:

\[ \text{jobs} + \text{income growth} + \text{public services} + \text{opportunity}. \]

And geography adds a third demand:

\[ \text{regional balance} + \text{connectivity} + \text{national cohesion}. \]

The best development model must somehow provide all three.

A Crisis of Confidence, Not Yet a Crisis of Growth

Indonesia today is not experiencing another 1997.

That distinction is important.

The banking system is different.

Foreign-currency vulnerabilities are different.

Public debt remains much lower.

The economy is still growing.

The country continues to attract investment.

There is no reason to describe the present situation as a conventional financial crisis.

But confidence can weaken long before a crisis appears.

That is why recent market signals matter.

Credit-rating outlooks do not tell us that collapse is inevitable.

Falling stock prices do not prove that the economy is fundamentally unsound.

A weaker rupiah does not mean Indonesia is repeating 1997.

But taken together, they tell us something about expectations.

Investors are becoming less certain that the institutional model they trusted for two decades will remain unchanged.

That is a different type of warning.

Indonesia’s 5 Percent Paradox

We can now return to the original puzzle.

How can Indonesia grow at more than 5 percent while investors become more pessimistic?

Because the two observations are answering different questions.

GDP asks:

How much did the economy produce?

Markets ask:

How confident are we about future returns, policy, and institutions?

Households ask:

Am I actually becoming more prosperous and secure?

And Indonesia’s geography asks:

Can development reach the rest of the archipelago rather than remaining concentrated in a few places?

These questions do not have to produce the same answer.

Indonesia may therefore be experiencing several realities at once:

\[ \text{strong headline growth}, \]

\[ \text{weakening household confidence}, \]

\[ \text{rising institutional uncertainty}, \]

and

\[ \text{persistent geographic imbalance}. \]

That combination is unusual, but not contradictory.

What Nusantara Really Represents

Nusantara belongs in this story not simply as an infrastructure project, but as a metaphor.

It represents the belief that Indonesia can deliberately reshape its economic geography.

That ambition is understandable.

But the slow development of the new capital also illustrates the limits of administrative solutions.

Economic concentration is sticky.

People move toward opportunity.

Firms move toward workers and markets.

Suppliers move toward firms.

Universities and professional services follow economic density.

That is why Java remains dominant.

The question is therefore not whether Nusantara “succeeds” or “fails” in a narrow sense.

The more interesting question is whether Indonesia can create multiple centers of economic gravity.

If it can, the country becomes more balanced.

If it cannot, political power may move while the economy remains where it was.

Indonesia’s Most Important Resource May Be Institutional

Indonesia still has enormous potential.

Few countries combine:

  • a population of more than 280 million;
  • major strategic mineral reserves;
  • a large domestic market;
  • a relatively young labor force;
  • and an increasingly important geopolitical position.

The country also possesses something less visible.

It has already demonstrated an ability to solve extraordinarily difficult coordination problems.

It created a national language.

It built a pluralist political framework across immense religious and ethnic diversity.

It decentralized without disintegrating.

It constructed democratic elections across thousands of islands.

It rebuilt macroeconomic credibility after one of the worst crises in its history.

Those achievements matter.

They suggest that Indonesia’s greatest strength may not be its nickel, coal, palm oil, copper, or gold.

It may be its ability to construct institutions capable of making an extremely difficult geography governable.

That is why the current debate matters so much.

The Deeper Lesson

Indonesia’s experience illustrates a broader lesson about development.

Economic growth is not only about accumulating capital.

It is also about maintaining institutions that allow people to trust the future.

A country can possess natural resources, factories, infrastructure, and a huge labor force.

But investment decisions depend partly on expectations.

Expectations depend partly on credibility.

And credibility is accumulated slowly.

It can also be lost surprisingly quickly.

That gives us a useful chain:

\[ \text{institutions} \rightarrow \text{credibility} \rightarrow \text{investment} \rightarrow \text{growth}. \]

But the relationship can also run in the other direction.

If growth fails to create broad prosperity, political pressure builds.

That pressure can produce demands for intervention.

Intervention can weaken confidence if institutions are perceived as becoming less predictable.

And Indonesia adds another layer:

\[ \text{difficult geography} \rightarrow \text{need for state coordination} \rightarrow \text{greater demands on institutions}. \]

So perhaps the fuller cycle is:

\[ \text{geography} \rightarrow \text{state-building} \rightarrow \text{growth} \rightarrow \text{distributional disappointment} \rightarrow \text{political pressure} \rightarrow \text{policy change} \rightarrow \text{institutional uncertainty} \rightarrow \text{weaker confidence}. \]

That is the tension Indonesia now appears to be navigating.

Its problem is not that development has stopped.

Its challenge is to prevent an understandable effort to make development faster, more inclusive, and more geographically balanced from weakening the institutional foundations that made sustained growth possible in the first place.

And that may be why Indonesia’s current moment is so interesting.

The country is still growing.

The resources are still there.

The opportunities are still enormous.

The geography is still difficult.

But Indonesia has spent decades proving that geography is not destiny.

The most important question now may be whether the institutions that overcame those geographic constraints can remain credible while the state asks them to do even more.

After the trauma of 1997 and two decades spent rebuilding confidence, Indonesia’s most valuable economic asset may not be buried underground or sitting in a new capital.

It may be something much less visible:

the belief that the rules of the game will remain credible.

If that belief holds, Indonesia has every reason to remain one of Asia’s most important growth stories.

If it weakens, 5 percent growth may no longer be enough to reassure anyone.

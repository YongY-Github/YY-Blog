The Evolution of Strategic Thought

A Brief History of Game Theory

Game theory is the study of strategic interaction — situations in which outcomes depend not only on one’s own choices, but also on the choices of others.

Today, it is used across:
- economics,
- politics,
- military strategy,
- biology,
- computer science,
- artificial intelligence,
- and even cryptocurrency design.

But the road toward modern game theory was surprisingly long.

Its development stretched from:
- ancient legal reasoning,
- to probability theory,
- to Cold War strategy,
- to modern algorithmic systems.

And along the way, it produced some of the most fascinating intellectual stories in modern science.

1. Ancient Intuitions and Early Foundations

Long before game theory became formal mathematics, people were already thinking strategically.

The Talmud and the Marriage Contract Problem

One of the earliest proto-game-theoretic discussions appears in the Talmud (roughly 400–500 CE).

The problem concerned:
- how to divide assets among claimants,
- particularly in marriage contracts under bankruptcy-like conditions.

Modern scholars later realized that these discussions resembled concepts from:
- cooperative game theory,
- bargaining,
- and fair division.

In a sense:

Rabbis were thinking about strategic allocation problems over a millennium before formal game theory existed.

2. The First Mixed Strategy: Waldegrave (1713)

The first known formal solution to a strategic game appears in a 1713 letter by James Waldegrave concerning a card game called Le Her. :contentReferenceoaicite:0

The game involved:
- bluffing,
- uncertainty,
- and probabilistic decisions.

Waldegrave proposed what we now call a:

mixed strategy

Instead of always choosing the same action, players should randomize.

This was revolutionary.

Fun Fact

Waldegrave’s solution appeared not in a journal article, but in a private letter sent to Pierre-Remond de Montmort.

Game theory was literally born through correspondence between gamblers and mathematicians.

3. Bernoulli and Utility (1738)

In 1738, Daniel Bernoulli introduced one of the foundational concepts of economics:

utility.

Using the famous St. Petersburg Paradox, Bernoulli argued that:
- the value of money is subjective,
- and depends on existing wealth.

This became the foundation of:
- expected utility theory,
- risk analysis,
- and modern decision theory.

4. Cournot and Strategic Competition (1838)

The French economist Antoine Augustin Cournot performed what is widely regarded as the first true game-theoretic economic analysis.

He studied a duopoly:
- two firms choosing output quantities while considering each other’s actions.

This produced the idea that:

Each firm’s optimal decision depends on expectations about the rival’s behavior.

Cournot had essentially discovered an early form of equilibrium analysis more than a century before Nash.

5. Darwin and Strategic Evolution

Surprisingly, evolutionary biology also contains implicit game theory.

In The Descent of Man (1871), Charles Darwin argued that natural selection would stabilize sex ratios.

Why?

Because if one gender became rare:
- that gender would gain reproductive advantage,
- pushing the population back toward balance.

This was strategic reasoning without formal mathematics.

6. Zermelo and Chess (1913)

In 1913, the German mathematician Ernst Zermelo proved one of the first formal theorems in game theory.

For games like chess:

  • either White has a winning strategy,
  • or Black has a winning strategy,
  • or both can force at least a draw.

This was profound because it suggested:

Strategic games possess underlying mathematical structure.

7. Émile Borel and Mixed Strategie

Between 1921 and 1927, the French mathematician Émile Borel developed the first formal treatment of mixed strategies.

Borel:
- formally defined probabilistic play,
- explored minimax reasoning,
- and investigated equilibrium existence.

Interestingly:

Borel initially believed minimax solutions might fail for sufficiently complex games.

He later admitted he could not prove this.

8. John von Neumann and the Birth of Modern Game Theory

That open question was answered in 1928 by John von Neumann.

He proved the famous:

Minimax Theorem

For every finite two-player zero-sum game:
- optimal mixed strategies exist.

This result effectively launched modern game theory.

Fun Fact: Von Neumann’s Mind

Wikipedia’s description of von Neumann almost sounds fictional.

He made major contributions to:
- quantum mechanics,
- set theory,
- economics,
- computer science,
- hydrodynamics,
- statistics,
- numerical analysis,
- and nuclear weapons research.

He was one of the last true universal mathematicians.

9. Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944)

In 1944, von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern published:

Theory of Games and Economic Behavior

This book formally established game theory as a scientific field.

Major contributions included:
- expected utility theory,
- normal-form games,
- extensive-form games,
- cooperative games,
- and rigorous strategic analysis.

It was one of the most influential economics books ever written.

10. John Nash and Strategic Equilibrium

In the early 1950s, John Nash transformed the field forever.

He introduced the:

Nash Equilibrium

A situation where:
- no player can improve by changing strategy alone.

Unlike earlier minimax theory, Nash’s framework applied to:
- many players,
- non-zero-sum games,
- and general strategic interaction.

This became the central solution concept in modern game theory.

Fun Fact: Nash Was Only 21

John Nash wrote his equilibrium paper as a PhD student in his early twenties.

The paper itself was astonishingly short.

One of the most influential ideas in economics emerged from only a few pages.

Another Fun Fact: “That’s Not Nash”

Many people recognize Nash from A Beautiful Mind.

But as the lecture slides humorously note:

“That’s not Nash. That’s Russell Crowe.” :contentReferenceoaicite:5

11. The Prisoner’s Dilemma

Around the same time, researchers at RAND Corporation:
- Merrill Flood,
- and Melvin Dresher,

developed the strategic structure later known as the:

Prisoner’s Dilemma.

Albert Tucker later popularized the framework using prison sentences.

The game revealed one of the deepest insights in social science:

Individually rational behavior can produce collectively irrational outcomes.

12. Kuhn and Extensive Form Games

In 1950 and 1953, Harold Kuhn formalized the modern structure of extensive-form games.

These models captured:
- timing,
- sequential decisions,
- information sets,
- and dynamic strategy.

Today, nearly all modern strategic models descend from Kuhn’s framework.

13. Lloyd Shapley and Cooperative Games

In 1953, Lloyd Shapley introduced the:

Shapley Value

A method for fairly allocating gains among participants in a coalition.

The idea became central in:
- bargaining theory,
- political coalitions,
- machine learning explainability,
- and market design.

Fun Fact: Shapley Value Is Used in AI Today

Modern explainable AI methods use “SHAP values,” directly inspired by Shapley’s cooperative game theory.

Game theory quietly became part of machine learning.

14. Schelling and Coordination

In 1960, Thomas Schelling introduced the idea of:

focal points

When communication is impossible, people often converge on psychologically “obvious” solutions.

For example:
- “Meet me in New York tomorrow.”

Where?

Most people intuitively think:
- Grand Central Station,
- noon.

This is not logic alone.

It is shared expectation.

15. Selten and Credibility

Reinhard Selten refined Nash equilibrium by introducing:

  • subgame perfect equilibrium,
  • trembling-hand perfection.

His work distinguished:
- credible threats,
- from empty threats.

This became foundational in:
- bargaining,
- political strategy,
- and industrial organization.

16. Harsanyi and Incomplete Information

John Harsanyi developed Bayesian games.

Players could now:
- possess private information,
- hold beliefs,
- and reason under uncertainty.

This transformed game theory into a framework for:
- auctions,
- signaling,
- political economy,
- and modern information economics.

17. Evolutionary Game Theory

In the 1970s and 1980s, John Maynard Smith applied game theory to biology.

He introduced the:

Evolutionarily Stable Strategy (ESS)

A strategy that cannot easily be invaded by mutants.

Game theory moved from:
- rational humans,
to:
- evolving populations.

18. Aumann and Repeated Games

Robert Aumann revolutionized repeated games and common knowledge.

He also introduced:
- correlated equilibrium,
- formal common knowledge,
- and deep analysis of long-run cooperation.

Fun Fact: “Agreeing to Disagree”

Aumann proved a famous theorem:

Rational people with common prior beliefs cannot “agree to disagree.”

If both are perfectly rational and fully informed about each other’s beliefs:
- persistent disagreement should disappear.

Whether this describes real humans is another matter.

19. Axelrod and Tit-for-Tat

In 1984, Robert Axelrod organized computer tournaments for repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma.

The surprising winner was:

Tit-for-Tat

The strategy:
- cooperates initially,
- then mirrors the opponent’s previous action.

Simple.
Forgiving.
Retaliatory.
Transparent.

It became one of the most famous strategic insights ever discovered.

20. Algorithmic Game Theory

In the 2000s, researchers such as Tim Roughgarden connected game theory with computer science.

This led to:
- algorithmic game theory,
- network congestion models,
- internet routing analysis,
- and the concept of the:

Price of Anarchy

How much efficiency is lost because individuals pursue self-interest?

21. Bitcoin and Strategic Incentives

In 2008, Satoshi Nakamoto published the Bitcoin whitepaper.

At its core, Bitcoin is deeply game-theoretic.

The system works because:
- miners are rewarded for honest participation,
- cheating becomes costly,
- and incentives align decentralized behavior.

In many ways:

Blockchain systems are large-scale economic games encoded in software.

Conclusion

The history of game theory is ultimately the history of strategic reasoning itself.

What began as:
- gambling mathematics,
- philosophical puzzles,
- and military strategy,

became a universal framework for understanding:
- competition,
- cooperation,
- conflict,
- bargaining,
- evolution,
- and intelligence.

Today, game theory influences:
- economics,
- politics,
- AI systems,
- online platforms,
- social networks,
- and cryptocurrency protocols.

And perhaps most importantly:

It changed how we think about thinking itself.

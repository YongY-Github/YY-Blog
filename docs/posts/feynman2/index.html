The Ice Water Test

On 28 January 1986, the space shuttle Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch.

Seven astronauts died.

The images became some of the most haunting in the history of the American space program: the white exhaust trails separating against a clear blue sky, debris falling toward the Atlantic, and millions of people watching in disbelief.

Among the crew was Christa McAuliffe, a schoolteacher who had been selected to become the first participant in NASA’s Teacher in Space Project.

The disaster immediately produced an enormous question:

How could one of the most technologically sophisticated organizations in the world have allowed this to happen?

A presidential commission was formed to investigate.

One of its members was physicist Richard Feynman.

And Feynman’s contribution to the investigation eventually came down to something almost absurdly simple:

a piece of rubber and a glass of ice water.

A Physicist Enters a Bureaucracy

Feynman was an unusual choice for a government commission.

He was a Nobel Prize-winning physicist at Caltech, famous for his work in quantum electrodynamics.

He was also famous for disliking pretension.

Committees, formalities, titles, and bureaucratic rituals did not impress him very much.

What interested him was:

What actually happened?

That sounds obvious.

But large organizations rarely encounter reality directly.

Reality passes through layers.

An engineer sees a physical problem.

A manager sees an engineering concern.

A director sees a risk assessment.

A senior executive sees a presentation slide.

A political leader sees a briefing.

At every stage, information is compressed.

Sometimes that compression is necessary.

No executive can personally inspect every bolt, seal, spreadsheet, or line of code.

But every layer creates an opportunity for something dangerous to happen:

The facts can become progressively less uncomfortable as they travel upward.

That was one of the deeper problems Feynman encountered.

Ask the Stupid Question

During the investigation, Feynman repeatedly asked basic questions.

What does this acronym mean?

How does this component work?

Why was this decision made?

What happens physically when the temperature falls?

People sometimes interpret this as evidence that Feynman knew nothing about rockets.

In one sense, that was precisely his advantage.

He did not carry all the assumptions that insiders had accumulated.

Experts develop shortcuts.

They learn which questions are considered settled.

They know what everyone in the organization is supposed to know.

They also learn what questions make meetings uncomfortable.

An outsider has fewer such constraints.

The outsider can ask:

“Why?”

And then:

“But why?”

And then:

“Can you show me?”

These can be surprisingly powerful questions.

Expertise Has a Blind Spot

Expertise is enormously valuable.

But expertise can also create blindness.

Once we have worked inside a system for years, we stop noticing its assumptions.

We begin saying things such as:

  • “That’s just how we do it.”
  • “The model has always worked.”
  • “The procedure has already been approved.”
  • “That’s within acceptable limits.”
  • “Management is aware of the issue.”
  • “The committee has reviewed it.”

Each statement may be technically true.

But none necessarily answers the most important question:

Does the thing actually work?

Feynman’s instinct was repeatedly to return from organizational language to physical reality.

Not:

What does the report say?

But:

What does the rubber do when it gets cold?

The O-Ring Problem

The solid rocket boosters on the shuttle were assembled from cylindrical segments.

At the joints between those segments were rubber seals known as O-rings.

Their task was simple but critical.

When the rocket ignited, extremely hot gases had to remain sealed inside the booster.

If the O-rings failed to seal quickly enough, those gases could escape.

Engineers had already observed evidence of O-ring erosion on previous shuttle flights.

And concerns had been raised about performance at low temperatures.

The morning Challenger launched was unusually cold.

That mattered because rubber becomes less resilient when cold.

If compressed rubber does not rebound quickly enough, the seal may not form properly at the moment it is needed.

This sounds technical.

Feynman made it visible.

Ice Water

During a televised hearing of the Rogers Commission, Feynman took a sample of O-ring material.

He compressed it with a clamp.

He placed it in ice water.

He waited.

Then he removed it and released the pressure.

The rubber did not immediately return to its original shape.

That was the demonstration.

No elaborate mathematical model.

No 150-page technical report.

No executive summary.

Just rubber.

Cold water.

And observation.

Feynman explained that at sufficiently low temperature the material had lost resilience.

The lesson was instantly understandable.

Cold rubber responds differently from warm rubber.

Suddenly, an abstract discussion of seals, launch conditions, and engineering tolerances became something anyone could see.

That was what made the demonstration so powerful.

The Difference Between Explanation and Demonstration

Large organizations are full of explanations.

Reports explain.

Slides explain.

Risk matrices explain.

Committees explain.

But explanations can become detached from whatever they are supposed to describe.

Feynman’s ice-water demonstration collapsed the distance.

Instead of saying:

“There may be reduced seal resiliency under low-temperature launch conditions.”

he effectively said:

“Look.”

This distinction matters enormously.

A claim can survive within an organization because it is expressed in sufficiently sophisticated language.

A demonstration is harder to negotiate with.

Nature Does Not Attend Meetings

Feynman’s deepest advantage during the investigation came from the culture of physics.

In science, nature has the final vote.

A theory may be elegant.

A famous scientist may believe it.

A committee may approve it.

A journal may publish it.

But if observation contradicts the theory, nature wins.

This produces a radically different attitude toward authority.

In ordinary organizations, hierarchy matters.

A junior engineer can be wrong because the vice-president says so.

In physics, the vice-president does not get a vote.

The rubber either rebounds or it does not.

A bridge either carries the load or it collapses.

A drug either produces the claimed effect or it does not.

A regression either supports the conclusion or it does not.

Reality is indifferent to organizational rank.

That sounds obvious.

Yet organizations regularly forget it.

How Truth Gets Diluted

Imagine an engineer writes:

Do not launch below this temperature.

That message travels upward.

A manager summarizes:

“There are concerns about seal performance at low temperatures.”

The next level reports:

“Temperature represents a manageable technical risk.”

Another meeting concludes:

“No evidence demonstrates that launch is unsafe.”

Eventually someone says:

“The launch remains within acceptable risk parameters.”

No single person necessarily lies.

That is what makes the process dangerous.

The truth can disappear without a conspiracy.

Each person merely translates the message into language appropriate for the next level.

But something is lost at every translation.

The original statement was:

Don’t launch.

The final statement becomes:

There is no conclusive reason not to launch.

Those are not the same proposition.

The Organizational Telephone Game

This happens far beyond NASA.

Consider a company developing software.

The engineer says:

“The system crashes under heavy load.”

The team leader says:

“We have some scalability issues.”

The director reports:

“Performance optimization remains ongoing.”

The executive presentation says:

“Platform scaling progressing according to roadmap.”

Then the product launches.

And crashes.

Or consider a research project.

The research assistant says:

“The result disappears when we change the specification.”

The principal investigator says:

“The coefficient is somewhat sensitive.”

The conference presentation says:

“Results are broadly robust.”

The published abstract says:

“We provide robust evidence that…”

Nobody needed to fabricate anything.

The language simply became more confident as it moved farther from the data.

The Higher You Rise, the Less Reality You See

This creates a paradox of leadership.

The more senior your position, the greater your authority.

But usually the less direct contact you have with the underlying reality.

A junior worker touches the machinery.

A programmer sees the code.

A nurse sees the patient.

A teacher sees the student.

A field researcher sees the data collection process.

Senior leaders see reports about those things.

Eventually the chief executive sees a dashboard.

Dashboards are useful.

But a dashboard is a representation of reality.

It is not reality itself.

This is why good leaders occasionally need to move downward through the information hierarchy.

Talk to the engineer.

Open the dataset.

Visit the classroom.

Try the product.

Read the complaint.

Observe the process.

Ask someone to demonstrate the thing everyone says is working.

In other words:

Bring the ice water.

Lesson 1: Never Confuse Process With Truth

One of the great institutional mistakes is believing that because the correct procedure was followed, the correct answer must have emerged.

It does not follow.

A process can be:

  • documented;
  • audited;
  • reviewed;
  • approved;
  • ISO-certified;
  • signed by twelve committees;

and still produce nonsense.

Procedure is valuable because it can make good outcomes more likely.

But procedure is not evidence that the outcome itself is good.

This distinction matters in universities as much as aerospace engineering.

A course can have:

  • learning outcomes;
  • curriculum maps;
  • assessment matrices;
  • rubrics;
  • moderation forms;
  • accreditation approval;

and students may still learn very little.

The documents describe education.

They are not education.

That connects directly to Feynman’s earlier critique of cargo cult science.

We can reproduce all the rituals associated with success while losing the causal mechanism that made those rituals useful.

Lesson 2: Ask for the Underlying Evidence

Whenever someone presents a conclusion, it is useful to ask:

What would I have to see to believe this myself?

Suppose someone tells you:

“Students prefer this teaching method.”

Ask:

What was the survey question?

Suppose they say:

“The policy increased employment.”

Ask:

Compared with what counterfactual?

Suppose someone says:

“The model is highly accurate.”

Ask:

Out of sample?

Suppose someone tells management:

“The project is 90 percent complete.”

Ask:

Show me what currently works.

None of this means distrusting experts.

It means distinguishing between:

trusting expertise

and

outsourcing judgment completely.

A healthy institution needs both expert delegation and mechanisms for verification.

Lesson 3: Encourage People to Say “This Is Not Safe”

Organizations usually claim that they welcome bad news.

Many do not.

The employee who repeatedly raises problems may be described as:

  • negative;
  • difficult;
  • uncooperative;
  • not strategic;
  • not a team player.

Over time, employees learn.

They stop bringing bad news.

This creates a dangerous equilibrium.

Senior management believes everything is going well because nobody reports serious problems.

Nobody reports serious problems because senior management has taught everyone not to.

Eventually reality breaks the equilibrium.

And management asks:

“Why didn’t anyone tell us?”

Usually someone did.

Lesson 4: Psychological Safety Is Not About Being Nice

Modern organizations talk extensively about psychological safety.

It is sometimes misunderstood as making everyone comfortable.

That is almost the opposite of what matters.

A psychologically safe organization is one in which someone can say:

“The boss is wrong.”

“The model doesn’t work.”

“The project is failing.”

“The data don’t support our conclusion.”

“This launch is unsafe.”

without destroying their career.

The purpose is not comfort.

The purpose is ensuring that uncomfortable information reaches decision-makers before reality delivers it more violently.

Lesson 5: Beware the Normalization of Deviance

There is another important concept associated with the Challenger disaster: normalization of deviance.

Suppose something occurs that violates expectations.

Nothing bad happens.

So the organization accepts it.

It happens again.

Still nothing bad happens.

Gradually, what was originally classified as abnormal becomes normal.

Imagine that a component shows slight damage after a mission.

Engineers worry.

But the mission succeeded.

Next time, similar damage appears.

Again, the mission succeeds.

After enough successful missions, the evidence of damage becomes psychologically reclassified:

Apparently this is normal.

But repeated survival does not prove safety.

It may simply mean that the system has repeatedly been lucky.

Twenty successful crossings of a cracked bridge do not demonstrate that the bridge is sound.

This is one of the most dangerous ways organizations learn the wrong lesson from experience.

Twenty-Four Successful Missions Prove What?

Before Challenger, NASA had completed many successful shuttle missions.

That record naturally produced confidence.

But success can be misleading.

Suppose a risky procedure has a 2 percent probability of catastrophe each time it is performed.

Most attempts will succeed.

A run of successful outcomes can therefore occur even when the underlying system is dangerously risky.

This is a basic statistical point.

Absence of disaster is not evidence of absence of risk.

Organizations, however, often update their beliefs differently.

“We did it last time and nothing happened.”

Soon becomes:

“We have always done it this way.”

Eventually:

“This method is proven safe.”

Past success becomes the justification for continuing the behavior that may eventually produce catastrophe.

Lesson 6: Incentives Shape What Organizations Believe

Why do organizations sometimes ignore obvious risks?

Not necessarily because people are stupid.

Incentives matter.

NASA faced pressure to demonstrate that the shuttle could operate frequently and reliably.

Managers had schedules.

Budgets mattered.

Political expectations mattered.

Prestige mattered.

Engineers were thinking:

Will the seal work?

Managers were also thinking:

What happens if we delay again?

These are different objective functions.

Neither side needs to be corrupt.

But once institutional incentives reward maintaining the schedule, evidence is interpreted through that objective.

This is an important principle in economics:

People respond to incentives—but organizations also interpret information through incentives.

When evidence threatens an important institutional goal, the standard required to accept that evidence often becomes mysteriously higher.

Lesson 7: Simplicity Can Defeat Bureaucratic Complexity

Feynman’s demonstration is memorable because it compressed an enormous technical argument into something observable.

This is a valuable skill.

When faced with a complicated claim, ask:

Is there a simple test that captures the central mechanism?

Economists do this with natural experiments.

Scientists do it with controlled experiments.

Programmers write minimal reproducible examples.

Doctors order diagnostic tests.

Engineers build prototypes.

Good teachers create simple examples.

The point is not that everything is simple.

Some systems genuinely are enormously complex.

The point is that complexity should not become a hiding place.

A powerful explanation often identifies the small mechanism underneath the large system.

Lesson 8: The Junior Person May Know More Than the Senior Person

Hierarchies allocate decision rights.

They do not necessarily allocate knowledge.

The person with the most information about a problem may be very low in the organization.

The mechanic knows what the machine sounds like.

The nurse notices the change in the patient.

The research assistant knows which observations were problematic.

The programmer knows where the workaround is hidden.

The teacher knows which students are lost.

The senior manager may know none of these things.

This means organizations face a fundamental problem:

How do we move local knowledge toward central decision-makers without destroying or filtering it along the way?

That is not merely a communication problem.

It is an institutional-design problem.

The Feynman Principle

Feynman’s contribution to the final Challenger report included a memorable conclusion:

“For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled.”

This may be one of the best sentences ever written about organizational management.

Replace nature with:

  • the market;
  • students;
  • patients;
  • voters;
  • customers;
  • data;
  • competitors;

and the principle still works.

You can manage perceptions for a while.

You can revise the presentation.

You can redefine the KPI.

You can explain why the target was technically achieved.

But eventually the underlying reality asserts itself.

The customer leaves.

The bridge collapses.

The student cannot perform the task.

The firm runs out of cash.

The model fails out of sample.

The rocket explodes.

Reality is patient.

But it eventually audits everything.

What This Means for Universities

The Challenger story has uncomfortable parallels in education.

Universities increasingly operate through systems of:

  • metrics;
  • rankings;
  • learning outcomes;
  • quality assurance;
  • publication targets;
  • accreditation;
  • student evaluations;
  • research KPIs.

Many of these systems are useful.

But they can create an illusion similar to NASA’s risk-management bureaucracy.

If the indicators look good, we begin assuming the underlying system must also be good.

A department can achieve excellent accreditation results while students struggle to think independently.

A researcher can accumulate publications while contributing little knowledge.

A course can satisfy every learning-outcome mapping requirement while producing mostly memorization.

At some point we need the educational equivalent of Feynman’s O-ring.

Ask the student:

Explain this idea to me.

No rubric.

No multiple-choice options.

No ChatGPT-generated paragraph.

Just:

What do you think is actually happening?

That may reveal more than another fifty pages of quality-assurance documentation.

And What About AI?

AI makes this problem even more interesting.

Organizations now receive increasingly polished summaries generated automatically.

Executives may soon encounter:

  • AI-generated dashboards;
  • AI-generated risk summaries;
  • AI-generated literature reviews;
  • AI-generated forecasts;
  • AI-generated policy briefs.

This could improve decision-making dramatically.

But it could also add another layer between decision-makers and reality.

Imagine:

\[ \text{Reality} \rightarrow \text{worker} \rightarrow \text{database} \rightarrow \text{AI summary} \rightarrow \text{manager} \rightarrow \text{executive}. \]

Every transformation potentially loses information.

And AI has a special danger:

it is exceptionally good at producing confident, coherent language.

The resulting explanation may sound cleaner than the underlying reality actually is.

This makes the Feynman principle increasingly valuable:

Show me the evidence.

Show me the raw data.

Show me the failure cases.

Show me what happens when we actually try it.

Bring the ice water.

The Most Difficult Question

It is easy to admire Feynman from a distance.

We know he was right.

History has already awarded the grades.

The harder question is what happens before everyone knows who is right.

Suppose you are in the meeting.

The deadline matters.

Everyone senior to you wants to proceed.

Your colleagues are silent.

The presentation looks reassuring.

You have evidence suggesting something is seriously wrong.

Do you speak?

That is harder.

Because truth-telling has costs.

You may appear naive.

You may annoy important people.

You may lose influence.

You may be excluded from future decisions.

You may even be wrong.

That last possibility matters.

Not every dissenter is Feynman.

Some difficult employees are simply difficult.

Some objections are mistaken.

Some risks genuinely are acceptable.

So the lesson cannot be:

Always trust the contrarian.

A better principle is:

Create institutions in which serious claims can be tested against evidence regardless of who makes them.

That is much closer to science.

Final Thought

The enduring image of the Challenger investigation is not a formula or an engineering diagram.

It is Richard Feynman sitting at a hearing table with a small piece of rubber and a glass of ice water.

Why has that image lasted?

Because it captures something fundamental.

Organizations speak in abstractions.

Reality speaks in consequences.

Between those two worlds sits a constant temptation to soften uncomfortable information, protect reputations, preserve schedules, maintain harmony, and tell ourselves that everything is probably fine.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes seven people die.

Feynman’s lesson was not that experts are useless or that bureaucracy is evil.

Large organizations require expertise.

They require procedures.

They require hierarchy.

They require abstraction.

The lesson is that none of these things can be allowed to become substitutes for reality.

Every organization therefore needs someone willing to ask:

What is actually happening?

Someone willing to look beneath the dashboard.

Someone willing to ask the embarrassing question.

Someone willing to test the assumption everyone else has stopped noticing.

Someone willing, occasionally, to bring a glass of ice water into the meeting.

Because procedures can be negotiated.

Reports can be rewritten.

Targets can be redefined.

And reputations can be managed.

But, as Feynman put it:

Nature cannot be fooled.

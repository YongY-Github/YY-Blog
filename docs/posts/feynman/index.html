Richard Feynman and the Illusion of Learning

Imagine being asked to evaluate a science textbook.

You receive hundreds of pages of explanations, diagrams, exercises, photographs, definitions, and equations. Your job is important: your recommendation may influence what thousands of children are taught.

So you carefully read the book.

Obviously.

Except Richard Feynman discovered something extraordinary when he served on a California textbook commission in the 1960s.

Apparently, not everyone was reading the books.

And one episode became almost absurd.

A publisher submitted material for a textbook that was not actually ready for inspection. Feynman found essentially blank pages where substantive material should have been.

Yet other evaluators still produced ratings.

Feynman was astonished.

How could you evaluate something you had not read?

The episode became part of a much larger criticism Feynman developed about education.

Schools, he believed, were very good at producing the appearance of knowledge.

Students learned terminology.

They memorized definitions.

They manipulated formulas.

They passed examinations.

But underneath all this activity was an uncomfortable question:

Did they actually understand anything?

“Energy Makes It Go”

One of Feynman’s favorite examples came from an elementary science textbook.

Imagine a picture of a wind-up toy.

The book asks:

What makes the toy move?

And gives an answer along the lines of:

Energy makes it move.

This sounds scientific.

It contains an important scientific word: energy.

But Feynman’s objection was devastatingly simple.

What has the child actually learned?

Suppose we replace energy with an invented word:

Wakalixes make it move.

Has our explanation become worse?

Not really.

Because we had not actually explained anything in the first place.

We merely attached a sophisticated label to something the child could already observe.

The toy moves.

Why?

Energy.

What is energy?

Well…

That is where the apparent explanation begins to collapse.

Feynman’s complaint was not that energy is meaningless. Energy is one of the most important concepts in physics.

His complaint was pedagogical.

Knowing the name of something is not the same as knowing something.

That distinction sounds obvious.

Yet much of education is built around forgetting it.

The Seduction of Vocabulary

Technical vocabulary is useful because experts need precise language.

But vocabulary can also hide ignorance.

Consider how easily this happens.

A student says:

“Inflation occurs because of inflationary pressures.”

A business consultant says:

“The company needs a more agile strategic ecosystem.”

A politician promises:

“Evidence-based, data-driven policy.”

A physicist says:

“Energy makes it go.”

Every sentence sounds impressive.

But ask one more question:

What exactly do you mean?

Suddenly things become uncomfortable.

Words that seemed to contain explanations sometimes turn out merely to be labels.

This was the kind of intellectual dishonesty Feynman disliked intensely.

And his target was not merely dishonest people.

The more dangerous possibility was honest people fooling themselves.

The First Principle

In 1974, Feynman delivered a famous commencement address at Caltech.

It became known as “Cargo Cult Science.”

He began with an unusual story.

During and after the Second World War, stories circulated about Pacific island communities attempting to reproduce aspects of the military infrastructure they had observed during wartime.

They constructed things resembling runways.

They imitated control towers.

They fashioned objects resembling headphones.

They reproduced the visible rituals associated with aircraft operations.

But the airplanes did not return.

Why?

Because copying the appearance of an airfield does not create the enormous invisible system that makes aviation work.

The runway was visible.

The logistics network was not.

The control tower was visible.

The industrial system producing aircraft was not.

The headphones were visible.

The radio network was not.

The outward form had been reproduced.

The causal mechanism had not.

Feynman saw a metaphor for bad science.

And, by extension, bad education.

We can reproduce all the visible rituals of knowledge without possessing the thing that made those rituals useful in the first place.

Then came one of Feynman’s most famous lines:

“The first principle is that you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool.”

That is the heart of the argument.

Cargo Cult Education

The idea extends naturally from science to education.

Consider what a university looks like.

There are:

  • lecture theatres;
  • textbooks;
  • examinations;
  • assignments;
  • grades;
  • learning outcomes;
  • accreditation documents;
  • degrees;
  • academic gowns.

These things accompany education.

But they are not education itself.

They are the runway lights.

The real question is whether something happens inside the student’s mind.

Does the student understand?

Can the student reason?

Can the student use an idea somewhere other than the examination in which it appeared?

Can the student explain why an answer is correct?

Can the student recognize when the same principle appears in an unfamiliar form?

If not, we may have constructed a beautiful educational runway.

But no aircraft are landing.

The Student Who Can Solve but Cannot Explain

This problem becomes especially interesting in mathematics, economics, statistics, and science.

Suppose a student knows that the elasticity of demand is

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{dQ}{dP}\frac{P}{Q}. \]

Excellent.

Now ask:

Why do we multiply the slope by (P/Q)?

Silence.

Or suppose a student estimates

\[ Y_i=\alpha+\beta_1X_i+u_i \]

and reports that \(\hat{\beta}_1=2.4\).

Ask:

What does 2.4 mean?

The student may know how to type the regression command.

They may know where the coefficient appears in the software output.

They may even know how to calculate a (t)-statistic.

But can they explain what the coefficient represents in ordinary language?

That is a different test.

And it is often a much harder one.

Memorizing the Shape of a Thought

There is a wonderful phrase in the source material:

“They had memorized the shape of the thought. They had not had the thought.”

That captures the problem beautifully.

A student can learn the outward structure of reasoning.

They learn:

  1. Write the formula.
  2. Substitute the numbers.
  3. Calculate the answer.
  4. Draw a box around it.

Everything looks correct.

But change the problem slightly and the structure collapses.

Why?

Because the student learned a procedure rather than a concept.

This is why a student can successfully calculate opportunity cost yet struggle to explain why the slope of a production possibilities frontier represents opportunity cost.

It is why students can calculate a regression coefficient without understanding conditional comparison.

It is why they can differentiate a function mechanically without knowing what a derivative tells them.

The ritual has been learned.

The mechanism has not.

Feynman in Brazil: The Same Problem

Feynman encountered an especially striking version of this problem while teaching physics in Brazil in the early 1950s.

Students could recite sophisticated definitions from their textbooks.

They knew technical vocabulary.

They could reproduce statements about light, optics, and other physical phenomena.

Yet Feynman discovered that many struggled when he connected those statements to ordinary experience.

The problem was not intelligence.

The students were capable.

The problem was the educational system.

They had learned that successful studying meant memorizing the correct sentences.

Feynman described students who could recite textbook material almost perfectly while struggling to recognize the same physics when it appeared outside the textbook.

This fascinated him.

They knew the words.

But the words had become detached from the world.

This Is Not an Argument Against Knowledge

There is an important distinction here.

Feynman was not arguing that students should stop memorizing things.

Some knowledge simply must be remembered.

You cannot reason intelligently about economics without knowing some economics.

You cannot understand physics while looking up every basic principle.

Vocabulary matters.

Facts matter.

Equations matter.

Memory matters.

The problem begins when recall is mistaken for understanding.

Knowing that the Battle of Hastings occurred in 1066 is knowledge.

Understanding why the Norman conquest transformed English political institutions is something more.

Knowing the formula for compound interest is knowledge.

Understanding why exponential growth eventually overwhelms linear growth is something more.

Knowing that omitted-variable bias exists is knowledge.

Understanding why leaving out a relevant confounder can alter a regression coefficient is something more.

Education needs both.

But examinations frequently measure the first because it is easier to measure.

The Forgetting Curve

This brings us to another famous figure: German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus.

In the nineteenth century, Ebbinghaus conducted painstaking experiments on memory, often using himself as the experimental subject.

He memorized lists of deliberately meaningless syllables and measured how quickly they disappeared from memory.

The result became known as the forgetting curve.

Without reinforcement, newly learned information tends to fade rapidly.

The exact rate varies enormously with the material, the learner, prior knowledge, retrieval practice, sleep, repetition and meaning. So the dramatic percentages sometimes circulated online—“you forget X percent after twenty minutes”—should not be treated as universal biological constants.

The broader insight, however, is robust:

Information that is merely crammed is fragile.

Anyone who has taught knows this.

Students can reproduce material beautifully on Friday and appear never to have encountered it by the following semester.

The examination recorded learning.

But what exactly did the examination measure?

Forgetting Is Not Necessarily Failure

Modern memory research adds an interesting twist.

Forgetting is not simply evidence that the brain is defective.

A useful cognitive system must discriminate.

Imagine remembering every:

  • license plate;
  • restaurant receipt;
  • advertisement;
  • conversation fragment;
  • password you no longer use;
  • sentence from every textbook;
  • face glimpsed on a train.

Perfect memory might be debilitating.

The brain needs to retain useful patterns while allowing irrelevant detail to fade.

This makes meaningful learning particularly important.

Isolated facts are vulnerable.

Knowledge connected to other knowledge is more durable.

An equation connected to intuition becomes easier to reconstruct.

A historical date connected to a causal narrative acquires meaning.

A statistical procedure connected to a real problem becomes something more than a sequence of commands.

Perhaps the goal is therefore not:

Remember everything.

It is:

Build enough understanding that important knowledge can be reconstructed.

The Feynman Test

This leads to the learning technique now commonly associated with Feynman’s name.

Take something you believe you understand.

Now explain it in simple language.

Imagine explaining it to an intelligent twelve-year-old.

No jargon unless you can explain the jargon.

No equations unless you can explain what every part of the equation is doing.

No hiding behind definitions copied from a textbook.

Try it with something apparently simple:

What is inflation?

Or:

Why does correlation not imply causation?

Or:

What exactly does a confidence interval tell us?

Or:

Why does comparative advantage create gains from trade even when one country is better at producing everything?

The moment the explanation becomes vague, stop.

You have found something valuable:

the boundary of your understanding.

That is not failure.

That is where learning begins.

Why Teaching Is Such a Powerful Test

This may explain why teaching is one of the best ways to discover whether we understand something.

Reading creates familiarity.

Familiarity creates confidence.

But explaining exposes structure.

When teaching, we suddenly encounter questions such as:

Why?

What does that actually mean?

Why does this assumption matter?

What happens if it doesn’t hold?

Can you give me an example?

These questions are merciless.

They force us beneath terminology.

Anyone who has taught the same course repeatedly has probably experienced this peculiar phenomenon: after explaining a concept for ten years, one student asks an innocent question and suddenly you realize that you had never completely thought through the answer.

That is not an embarrassment.

That is education.

For the teacher.

AI Makes Feynman’s Warning More Important

There is now a new twist that Feynman never encountered.

Generative AI can produce the appearance of understanding extraordinarily well.

Ask an AI system to explain an advanced concept and within seconds it can produce:

  • definitions;
  • examples;
  • equations;
  • analogies;
  • citations;
  • summaries;
  • and polished prose.

For education, this is enormously useful.

It is also potentially dangerous.

Because students can now produce the shape of a thought without necessarily having the thought.

A beautifully written essay may conceal almost no understanding.

A correct program may have been generated by someone who cannot explain what the code does.

A sophisticated regression analysis may have been produced by someone who cannot interpret the coefficient.

AI therefore makes Feynman’s distinction more important, not less.

The educational question can no longer be simply:

Can the student produce the answer?

Machines can increasingly do that.

The better question is:

Can the student interrogate, explain, modify, defend, and apply the answer?

That is much closer to understanding.

Perhaps Our Exams Are Testing the Wrong Thing

This has uncomfortable implications for education.

If a student can memorize material for an examination and forget it immediately afterward, what exactly have we measured?

If AI can answer the same examination perfectly, what human capability was the examination designed to assess?

Perhaps assessment needs to move toward questions that require students to:

  • explain;
  • compare;
  • diagnose;
  • apply;
  • criticize;
  • modify;
  • predict;
  • and defend.

Instead of:

“Define omitted-variable bias.”

Ask:

“Here are two regression results. Why did the coefficient change when the second variable was added?”

Instead of:

“State the definition of price elasticity.”

Ask:

“A restaurant raises prices by 10 percent and revenue increases. What can you infer about demand, and why?”

Instead of:

“What is Nash equilibrium?”

Ask:

“Why might individually rational behavior produce an outcome that makes everyone worse off?”

The difference is subtle but fundamental.

One asks students to reproduce knowledge.

The other asks them to use it.

The Blank Textbook Is Still With Us

Feynman’s textbook story is funny because it seems ridiculous.

Adults evaluating pages they have not properly read.

Scientific vocabulary masquerading as explanation.

Publishers producing materials optimized to satisfy committees.

Students learning definitions that disappear immediately after the examination.

But perhaps that is why the story remains powerful.

The blank textbook is a metaphor.

Whenever appearance substitutes for substance, another blank textbook appears.

Whenever jargon substitutes for explanation, we build another bamboo antenna.

Whenever a credential substitutes for competence, we illuminate another runway on which no aircraft can land.

And whenever we convince ourselves that we understand something merely because the words look familiar, we become members of Feynman’s committee.

Final Thought

Feynman’s deepest lesson was not about textbooks.

It was about intellectual honesty.

The greatest danger is not ignorance.

Ignorance can be corrected.

The greater danger is believing that we understand when we do not.

That is why the ability to say

“I don’t understand this yet.”

is not evidence of intellectual weakness.

It may be the beginning of genuine understanding.

Education should therefore do more than fill students with information.

It should cultivate curiosity, explanation, skepticism, experimentation, and the willingness to ask one more question.

Not:

What is this called?

But:

How does it actually work?

Not:

Can I reproduce the formula?

But:

Do I understand why it is true?

And perhaps, most importantly:

Am I sure I am not fooling myself?

That question mattered when Richard Feynman inspected school textbooks sixty years ago.

In an age of generative AI, it matters even more.

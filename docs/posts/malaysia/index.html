Malaysia Is Almost High Income. So Why Does It Still Feel Stuck?

Vision 2020, the Middle-Income Trap, and the Hardest Stage of Economic Development

Malaysia
Southeast Asia
economic development
middle-income trap
Author

-YY-

Published

October 11, 2026

Malaysia is in an unusual position.

On paper, the country is doing many things right.

Growth remains respectable—and recently, better than respectable. Malaysia’s economy grew 5.2 percent in 2025, and growth strengthened to 6.0 percent year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, according to Bank Negara Malaysia. (World Bank, 2026; Bank Negara Malaysia, 2026)

Its infrastructure is better than that of many countries at a similar income level.

The electronics industry remains globally important.

Malaysia is deeply integrated into international trade and sits along one of the world’s most important shipping routes.

And by conventional measures, the country is now very close to high-income status.

The World Bank’s current high-income threshold is US$14,375 in GNI per capita. Malaysia’s 2025 Atlas-method GNI per capita was US$12,380. (World Bank Country Classification, FY2027; World Bank Data)

So the statistical finish line is visible.

But the deeper transformation is less clear.

Yet there is still a sense that Malaysia has not quite completed the transformation that once seemed inevitable.

That is the puzzle.

In 1991, Prime Minister Mahathir Mohamad announced Vision 2020.

The ambition was bold but clear:

Malaysia would become a fully developed country by the year 2020.

At the time, the goal did not seem absurd.

Malaysia was growing rapidly.

Factories were expanding.

Foreign investment was pouring in.

New highways, industrial estates, ports, and urban centers were being built.

Between the late 1980s and mid-1990s, economic growth was extraordinarily strong.

Malaysia looked like one of Asia’s next great development successes.

Then 2020 arrived.

Malaysia was far richer than it had been in 1991.

Poverty was much lower.

The industrial base was deeper.

The country was far more urbanized.

The middle class was larger.

But the symbolic finish line had not been crossed.

And even now, with Malaysia close to the World Bank’s high-income threshold, the more important question remains unresolved:

Has Malaysia become the kind of high-productivity economy that Vision 2020 imagined?

That is a harder question than whether GNI per capita crosses a statistical line.

Malaysia’s Earlier Model Worked Extremely Well

To understand why Malaysia now seems stuck, we first have to acknowledge how successful the earlier model was.

In the 1970s and 1980s, Malaysia was still heavily dependent on commodities such as rubber, tin, palm oil, and petroleum.

The government understood that commodities alone would not generate sustained high-income development.

So Malaysia did something very sensible.

It industrialized through foreign investment.

Free-trade zones were established.

Penang became one of the most important early electronics hubs in Southeast Asia.

Multinational firms such as Intel, AMD, and Hewlett-Packard invested.

Malaysia offered:

  • relatively low labor costs;
  • political stability;
  • English-speaking workers;
  • good infrastructure;
  • investment incentives;
  • access to global shipping routes.

The strategy worked.

Workers moved out of low-productivity agriculture into manufacturing.

Productivity rose.

Exports expanded.

Urbanization accelerated.

Income increased quickly.

The development logic was powerful:

\[ \text{agriculture} \rightarrow \text{manufacturing} \rightarrow \text{higher productivity} \rightarrow \text{higher income}. \]

This is one of the classic ways economies move from low income to middle income.

And Malaysia executed that transition very successfully.

Penang and the Electronics Miracle

Penang became the symbol of this transformation.

It attracted multinational electronics firms and developed a dense manufacturing ecosystem.

Malaysia became deeply embedded in semiconductor supply chains.

Today, the country remains especially strong in assembly, testing, and packaging.

That is not a trivial achievement.

Malaysia accounts for roughly 7 percent of global semiconductor trade and around 13 percent of global semiconductor assembly, testing, and packaging. MIDA also notes that six of the world’s twelve largest semiconductor companies operate in Malaysia. In 2023 alone, Malaysia’s electrical and electronics sector attracted RM85.4 billion in approved manufacturing investment. (MIDA, 2024)

The difficulty is what comes next.

Semiconductor production is not one activity.

It is a value chain.

At one end are lower-margin activities such as assembly and testing.

At the other are higher-margin activities such as:

  • chip design;
  • intellectual property;
  • advanced fabrication;
  • specialized equipment;
  • advanced materials;
  • software;
  • research and development.

Malaysia became very good at one part of the chain.

The harder step was moving upward.

That distinction matters because economic development eventually becomes a question not just of what you produce, but where in the value chain you sit.

The Hard Part Comes After Industrialization

Moving from agriculture into factories can generate very large productivity gains.

Moving from factories into innovation is much harder.

The first transition often depends on:

  • infrastructure;
  • basic education;
  • foreign investment;
  • labor availability;
  • export access.

The second transition depends much more on:

  • advanced skills;
  • research;
  • entrepreneurship;
  • technological capability;
  • intellectual property;
  • high-quality institutions;
  • competitive domestic firms.

So the development problem changes.

The early stage looks like:

\[ \text{low-wage labor} + \text{foreign capital} \rightarrow \text{rapid catch-up}. \]

The later stage looks more like:

\[ \text{skills} + \text{technology} + \text{innovation} + \text{institutions} \rightarrow \text{productivity growth}. \]

Malaysia mastered much of the first formula.

The second has proved more difficult.

Too Expensive to Be Cheap, Not Yet Advanced Enough to Be Expensive

This is the classic middle-income problem.

As wages rise, low-cost manufacturing becomes harder to sustain.

Firms begin comparing Malaysia with countries such as Vietnam and Indonesia.

Those countries may offer lower wages and large labor forces.

At the same time, Malaysia is competing upward against economies such as South Korea, Taiwan, Japan, Singapore, and increasingly China.

Those countries compete through technology, productivity, design, capital intensity, and innovation.

Malaysia therefore risks getting squeezed between two groups:

\[ \text{lower-cost competitors} \quad\text{and}\quad \text{higher-productivity competitors}. \]

That is the uncomfortable middle.

If wages remain low, households feel stuck.

If wages rise without productivity, firms may leave.

The only durable solution is:

\[ \text{higher wages} \rightarrow \text{higher productivity}. \]

That requires upgrading.

The World Bank’s 2026 Malaysia Economic Monitor frames the problem in very similar terms: Malaysia is not scaling enough high-productivity firms and high-wage jobs. In manufacturing, the most productive “frontier” firms accounted for about 36 percent of employment in 2010, but only around 33 percent by 2023. (World Bank, 2026)

That is a subtle but important sign.

Malaysia has not stopped producing sophisticated goods.

The problem is that the most productive parts of the economy are not expanding fast enough to pull the rest of the labor market upward.

Why Didn’t Malaysia Move Up Faster?

This is where the story becomes more complicated.

There is no single explanation.

It would be tempting to blame one policy, one political era, or one institutional failure.

But Malaysia’s slower upgrading appears to reflect several interacting forces:

  • weak productivity growth;
  • shortages of high-level technical skills;
  • brain drain;
  • reliance on low-wage migrant labor;
  • limited innovation among domestic firms;
  • uneven competition;
  • political constraints on reform;
  • and a policy system shaped by long-standing distributional compromises.

The challenge is not that Malaysia lacks resources.

It is that the incentives for transformation have often been weaker than the incentives for maintaining a successful existing model.

The New Economic Policy: Success and Lock-In

One of the most important institutions in modern Malaysia is the New Economic Policy, introduced in 1971.

Its origins were serious.

Malaysia had experienced severe racial tensions in 1969.

Economic inequality overlapped with ethnicity.

The ethnic Chinese minority was disproportionately represented in private business and wealth, while many ethnic Malays, or Bumiputera, remained rural and poor.

The government responded with an explicit redistribution strategy.

The goals included:

  • poverty reduction;
  • greater Malay participation in education;
  • greater access to professional employment;
  • ownership and business opportunities;
  • and a reduction in the economic identification of ethnicity with occupation.

In important ways, the policy succeeded.

A much larger Malay middle class emerged.

Educational access expanded.

Poverty fell dramatically.

Social stability improved.

This matters.

The NEP should not be understood simply as an economic distortion.

It was also a political settlement.

But policies that solve one historical problem can create new difficulties when they become permanent.

Over time, preferences and quotas became embedded in:

  • public employment;
  • university admissions;
  • procurement;
  • licensing;
  • corporate ownership;
  • business opportunities.

That creates an institutional dilemma.

A policy can simultaneously be:

\[ \text{socially stabilizing} \]

and

\[ \text{economically distortionary}. \]

Both can be true.

The important question is whether an institution that was highly valuable at one stage of development remains equally well designed for another.

From Redistribution to Rent-Seeking

The concern is not simply that preferences exist.

The deeper danger is what happens when distributional policy becomes entangled with political connections.

Government contracts can become less competitive.

Licenses can become rents.

Corporate privileges can become difficult to remove.

Protected firms can become less exposed to market discipline.

Political access can become more valuable than innovation.

This is how redistribution can gradually become entangled with crony capitalism.

The mechanism is familiar:

\[ \text{protection} \rightarrow \text{lower competitive pressure} \rightarrow \text{weaker incentives to innovate}. \]

Again, this is not unique to Malaysia.

Many countries develop institutions that were initially useful but become harder to reform precisely because groups learn how to benefit from them.

That is institutional lock-in.

The Brain Drain Problem

Malaysia also faces a persistent problem with skilled emigration.

The precise current number is difficult to measure. But the scale is not trivial: the World Bank estimated Malaysia’s overseas-born diaspora at about 1 million people as far back as 2010, while explicitly warning that data limitations make exact measurement difficult. (World Bank, 2011)

Many educated Malaysians work abroad.

Singapore is the obvious destination because it is nearby, culturally familiar, English-speaking, and offers much higher salaries in many professional occupations.

Australia, the United Kingdom, the United States, and other countries also attract Malaysian talent.

Migration itself is not necessarily bad.

People gain experience.

Diaspora networks can create trade, investment, and knowledge links.

The problem appears when leaving becomes systematically more attractive than staying.

Suppose a talented graduate faces two options:

\[ \text{stay} = \text{lower salary} + \text{limited advancement} + \text{institutional frustration} \]

versus

\[ \text{leave} = \text{higher salary} + \text{stronger career path} + \text{more merit-based opportunities}. \]

Then emigration becomes rational at the individual level.

But repeated across thousands of people, it becomes a national development problem.

This creates a feedback loop:

\[ \text{limited high-quality jobs} \rightarrow \text{skilled emigration} \rightarrow \text{smaller talent pool} \rightarrow \text{harder industrial upgrading}. \]

A country can therefore invest heavily in education yet fail to capture the full return.

Cheap Labor as a Substitute for Upgrading

Another important issue is Malaysia’s long reliance on low-wage migrant labor.

Workers from Indonesia, Bangladesh, Nepal, and other countries have played major roles in:

  • manufacturing;
  • plantations;
  • construction;
  • services.

The World Bank has estimated that foreign workers make up roughly 15 percent of Malaysia’s labor force, concentrated disproportionately in lower-skilled occupations. It also notes that 65.8 percent of migrant workers had only primary education or less in 2022. (World Bank, 2020; World Bank, 2024)

For firms, this often makes economic sense.

Why spend heavily on automation if labor remains relatively cheap?

Why redesign production if workers can be added instead?

Why raise wages significantly if migrant labor remains available?

This may slow capital deepening, although the World Bank is careful to note that the automation effect has not yet been causally established for Malaysia itself. (World Bank, Migration, Automation, and the Malaysian Labor Market)

The mechanism is simple:

\[ \text{cheap labor} \rightarrow \text{weaker automation incentives} \rightarrow \text{slower productivity growth}. \]

The result is another trap.

Firms remain competitive because labor stays cheap.

But wages stay low partly because firms do not become much more productive.

The economy then struggles to generate the high-paying technical jobs that educated workers expect.

The Graduate Wage Problem

This matters because the social meaning of economic development changes over time.

In an earlier generation, moving from agriculture into factory work could represent a large improvement.

For a university graduate today, the benchmark is different.

A graduate may expect:

  • professional work;
  • career progression;
  • higher wages;
  • housing affordability;
  • financial security.

If starting salaries remain low relative to the cost of living, frustration grows.

The macroeconomy can look healthy while young professionals feel squeezed.

This creates another important distinction:

\[ \text{GDP growth} \neq \text{felt prosperity}. \]

Malaysia may continue growing.

But if skilled workers do not see strong wage progression, the economy can still feel stuck.

Vision 2020 Was Really About More Than Income

This is why the Vision 2020 story is so useful.

Mahathir’s original ambition was not simply:

Raise GNI per capita above a World Bank threshold.

The idea of becoming “developed” was broader.

A developed Malaysia was supposed to be:

  • technologically capable;
  • confident;
  • industrially sophisticated;
  • innovative;
  • cohesive;
  • internationally competitive.

So even if Malaysia crosses the statistical high-income line soon, the more interesting question remains:

Has the structure of the economy changed enough?

A country can become high income because:

  • the currency strengthens;
  • nominal incomes rise;
  • commodity prices help;
  • productivity improves gradually.

But that is not the same as becoming an innovation-led economy.

This is why the distinction matters:

\[ \text{high-income classification} \neq \text{fully transformed economy}. \]

Politics and the Cost of Reform

Malaysia’s political system has also made structural reform difficult.

For decades, Barisan Nasional dominated politics.

That produced predictability.

It also produced entrenched networks of patronage.

Then came the 1MDB scandal.

A state investment vehicle intended to support development became associated with one of the largest financial scandals in recent history.

The episode damaged trust.

In 2018, voters removed the ruling coalition from power for the first time.

That was a major democratic shift.

But political competition then became much more fragmented.

Malaysia went through multiple prime ministers and unstable coalitions over a relatively short period.

This matters for economic policy.

Structural reform is politically costly.

Governments may need to:

  • reduce subsidies;
  • broaden taxes;
  • reform procurement;
  • change labor policy;
  • redesign ethnic preferences;
  • increase competition;
  • restructure state-owned firms.

These reforms create obvious losers in the short run.

A fragile coalition has less incentive to undertake them.

That creates another form of lock-in:

\[ \text{political fragility} \rightarrow \text{reform avoidance} \rightarrow \text{structural persistence}. \]

The economy does not collapse.

It simply changes more slowly than it needs to.

The 1MDB Lesson

The 1MDB episode is also important for another reason.

Malaysia’s development model has historically involved a strong state.

State-linked investment can be very effective.

Governments can coordinate infrastructure.

They can support industrial policy.

They can take risks private investors avoid.

But state capacity works only when paired with governance.

The lesson is not:

government involvement is bad.

The lesson is:

\[ \text{state capacity} - \text{accountability} = \text{risk of rent extraction}. \]

That matters because Malaysia’s future development strategy will still require active government.

The semiconductor transition.

Energy policy.

Skills.

Research.

Infrastructure.

All require coordination.

So the challenge is not to withdraw the state.

It is to make the state more capable and more accountable at the same time.

The Semiconductor Opportunity Is Not Over

Malaysia’s story is not finished.

In fact, the global semiconductor reorganization may create one of the country’s best opportunities in decades.

Supply chains are diversifying.

Geopolitical tensions are pushing firms to reduce concentration.

Malaysia already possesses:

  • semiconductor experience;
  • industrial infrastructure;
  • logistics;
  • engineering talent;
  • multinational relationships;
  • political stability relative to many peers.

That is a strong starting point.

Malaysia is now trying to turn that existing advantage into a higher-value semiconductor strategy. The government has backed its National Semiconductor Strategy with an initial RM25 billion in public support, explicitly targeting deeper capabilities rather than simply more assembly. (MIDA, 2024)

The question is whether Malaysia can use its existing back-end strength to move further into:

  • design;
  • advanced packaging;
  • equipment;
  • materials;
  • research;
  • specialized engineering.

This is important because Malaysia does not need to become Taiwan overnight.

Development is not binary.

The question is whether the domestic share of value added rises.

In other words:

\[ \text{more chips} \]

is less important than

\[ \text{more Malaysian value inside each chip}. \]

That is the upgrading challenge.

Malaysia’s Position Is Better Than the Word “Trap” Suggests

The phrase middle-income trap can be misleading.

It sounds as though Malaysia is trapped permanently.

It is not.

Malaysia has many advantages:

  • strong infrastructure;
  • high literacy;
  • relatively good institutions;
  • global manufacturing integration;
  • English language capability;
  • natural resources;
  • strategic geography;
  • a sophisticated financial system;
  • a large urban middle class.

The country is not stuck because development is impossible.

It is stuck because the next step is much harder than the previous one.

Moving from low productivity to moderate productivity can be achieved partly by reallocating workers.

Moving from moderate productivity to high productivity requires innovation.

That is a qualitatively different challenge.

Crossing the Line May Hide the Problem

This creates a slightly ironic possibility.

Malaysia may soon cross into high-income status.

The numerical gap is not huge: 2025 GNI per capita was US$12,380, compared with the current high-income threshold of US$14,375.

When that happens, headlines will celebrate.

And they should.

It would represent enormous progress over several generations.

But the danger is that the classification itself becomes another source of complacency.

If Malaysia crosses the threshold before solving:

  • weak wage growth;
  • brain drain;
  • low innovation;
  • dependence on migrant labor;
  • uneven regional development;
  • productivity stagnation;

then the label “high income” may conceal unfinished work.

The country could statistically arrive before structurally arriving.

That would be a very Malaysian version of the middle-income trap:

escaping the category without fully escaping the underlying problem.

National Income Can Hide Regional Differences

There is another reason to be cautious about national averages.

Malaysia is geographically uneven.

The World Bank’s 2026 subnational work makes the point starkly: only 5 of Malaysia’s 16 states and federal territories currently meet the World Bank’s high-income threshold. Average incomes in Kuala Lumpur exceed those of Portugal, while Kelantan’s are roughly comparable to Sri Lanka’s and almost eight times lower than Kuala Lumpur’s. In Kelantan and Terengganu, more than 4 in 10 workers are employed below their skill level. (World Bank, 2026)

So a national high-income classification does not mean every part of Malaysia suddenly experiences high-income living standards.

This creates another distinction:

\[ \text{national high income} \neq \text{uniform high income}. \]

Development is always partly spatial.

The question is not only whether average income rises.

It is whether productivity and opportunity spread.

The Comfortable Road

The podcast makes one provocative argument that is worth keeping, though in a softer form.

Malaysia may have spent too long taking the comfortable road.

Its earlier model remained good enough.

Foreign firms kept investing.

Cheap labor remained available.

Commodities generated revenue.

Infrastructure remained strong.

The economy kept growing.

Nothing forced a dramatic change.

That is precisely why structural stagnation can persist.

Crisis forces reform.

Comfort often delays it.

The mechanism might look like:

\[ \text{good enough performance} \rightarrow \text{weak reform pressure} \rightarrow \text{slow upgrading}. \]

This is not failure.

It is inertia.

And inertia is especially dangerous because it feels stable.

Malaysia Didn’t Fail. It Stopped Transforming Fast Enough.

That, to me, is the fairest interpretation.

Malaysia did not fail.

It transformed dramatically.

A commodity-based economy became a manufacturing economy.

A poor country became an upper-middle-income country.

Infrastructure improved.

Education expanded.

Poverty fell.

Cities modernized.

Industrial capability deepened.

That is a remarkable achievement.

The issue is that the pace of transformation slowed before the process was complete.

The country reached the difficult final stage:

\[ \text{industrial economy} \rightarrow \text{innovation economy}. \]

That transition has taken longer than Vision 2020 imagined.

What Would the Next Malaysia Look Like?

The answer is not simply:

more factories.

Malaysia already has factories.

It is not simply:

more foreign investment.

Malaysia already attracts foreign investment.

The next stage requires something more difficult.

It requires more domestic capability.

More engineering.

More research.

More technology.

More competitive firms.

More entrepreneurship.

Higher productivity.

Higher wages.

And perhaps most importantly, stronger incentives for talented Malaysians to build their careers at home.

The development strategy therefore has to shift from:

\[ \text{attract foreign production} \]

toward:

\[ \text{build domestic capability}. \]

That is a very different objective.

What Vision 2020 Still Gets Right

Vision 2020 missed its deadline.

But the underlying ambition remains relevant.

The point was never simply to become richer.

It was to become a different kind of economy.

That challenge remains.

Malaysia is close enough to high-income status that the statistical question may soon disappear.

The structural question will not.

Can Malaysia move beyond assembly?

Can it retain more talent?

Can wages rise with productivity?

Can migrant labor become a complement to upgrading rather than a substitute for it?

Can affirmative-action institutions evolve without destabilizing the social settlement they helped create?

Can politics become stable enough to support difficult reform without returning to the old patterns of patronage?

Can Malaysia turn its semiconductor position into deeper technological capability?

These are not questions about whether Malaysia is successful.

It clearly is.

They are questions about what kind of success comes next.

The Hardest Part of Development

The early stages of development can be dramatic.

Workers leave farms.

Factories open.

Exports surge.

Cities grow.

Highways appear.

Living standards rise visibly.

The later stages are less spectacular.

They depend on:

  • institutions;
  • skills;
  • research;
  • competition;
  • incentives;
  • governance;
  • productivity.

These things are slower.

They are politically harder.

They cannot be imported as easily as factories.

That may be the deepest lesson of Malaysia’s story.

Malaysia did not run out of advantages.

It ran into the hardest stage of development.

And that is why the country’s current position is so interesting.

The finish line is close enough to see.

But perhaps the finish line was never really the World Bank threshold.

Malaysia’s challenge may no longer be crossing the high-income line.

It is building the kind of economy that makes crossing that line meaningful.

What Happens When the Fed Raises Interest Rates?

Economics
Monetary Policy
Author

-YY-

Published

September 17, 2026

On September 16, 2026, the U.S. Federal Reserve raised its target range for the federal funds rate by 0.25 percentage point, to 3.75–4.00%.

Why does a change in one short-term interest rate matter so much?

Because interest rates affect decisions throughout the economy: whether households borrow or save, whether firms invest, how investors value bonds and other assets, and even how international capital moves between countries.

The basic idea is simple:
Higher interest rates increase the price of borrowing and the return to saving, thereby reducing spending and cooling demand.

But the effects spread much further than this.

1. Exchange Rates: Why the U.S. Dollar May Appreciate

Suppose U.S. interest rates rise relative to interest rates in other countries.

Dollar-denominated financial assets then become relatively more attractive to international investors.

To purchase U.S. Treasury securities, bank deposits, or other U.S. assets, foreign investors need U.S. dollars.

This increases the demand for dollars:

\[ i_{US}\uparrow \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{Demand for dollar assets}\uparrow \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{Demand for US dollars}\uparrow \]

which tends to produce:

\[ \text{US dollar appreciation}. \]

There are, however, two important qualifications.

First, what matters is the interest-rate differential between the United States and other countries, not simply the U.S. interest rate by itself.

Second, financial markets are forward-looking. If investors already expected the rate increase, some or even most of the exchange-rate adjustment may occur before the Federal Reserve actually announces its decision.

2. Inflation: How Higher Interest Rates Reduce Price Pressures

One purpose of raising interest rates is to reduce inflationary pressure.

The most important mechanism is through aggregate demand.

Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive. As a result:

  • Households may postpone buying cars, houses, and other durable goods.
  • Mortgage and consumer-credit costs rise.
  • Firms may postpone investment projects.
  • Credit growth tends to slow.

Thus:

\[ i\uparrow \Rightarrow C\downarrow,\ I\downarrow \Rightarrow AD\downarrow \]

A slowing economy reduces pressure on prices and wages:

\[ AD\downarrow \Rightarrow \text{Inflationary pressure}\downarrow. \]

There may also be an exchange-rate channel.

If higher U.S. interest rates strengthen the dollar, imported goods become cheaper in dollar terms:

\[ \$\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Import prices}\downarrow \Rightarrow \text{Imported inflation}\downarrow. \]

Finally, monetary policy also works through expectations. If households and firms believe that the Federal Reserve is committed to controlling inflation, this may influence wage bargaining and price-setting behavior.

3. Bond Prices: Why They Fall When Interest Rates Rise

Bond prices and market interest rates generally move in opposite directions:

\[ i\uparrow \Rightarrow P_{\text{bond}}\downarrow. \]

Suppose an existing bond pays a fixed coupon of $40 per year.

If newly issued bonds now offer higher yields, investors will no longer be willing to pay the same price for the old bond.

The price of the existing bond must therefore fall until its yield becomes competitive with current market rates.

The basic bond-pricing relationship is:

\[ P = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{C}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^T}, \]

where:

  • (P) is the bond price,
  • (C) is the coupon payment,
  • (F) is the face value,
  • (y) is the market yield.

As (y) rises, the present value of future payments falls.

One important qualification is that the Federal Reserve directly controls a very short-term policy rate. Longer-term Treasury yields also depend on expected future interest rates, expected inflation, and risk or term premiums. Therefore, a 0.25 percentage point increase in the federal funds rate does not necessarily cause every market interest rate to rise by exactly 0.25 percentage point.

4. Cooling the Economy

Higher interest rates affect economic activity through several channels.

4.1 Household Consumption

Higher borrowing costs discourage interest-sensitive consumption.

This is particularly important for:

  • Housing
  • Cars
  • Appliances
  • Other durable goods financed with credit

Thus:

\[ i\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Borrowing cost}\uparrow \Rightarrow C\downarrow. \]

4.2 Business Investment

Higher interest rates also increase the cost of financing investment.

Some projects that were profitable at a low interest rate may no longer be worthwhile when financing becomes more expensive.

Thus:

\[ i\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Cost of capital}\uparrow \Rightarrow I\downarrow. \]

In the short run, this is primarily an aggregate-demand effect because investment is part of aggregate demand:

\[ AD=C+I+G+NX. \]

Over the longer run, however, persistently lower investment can also slow capital accumulation and therefore reduce the growth of productive capacity.

So it is useful to distinguish:

  • Short run: lower investment reduces aggregate demand.
  • Longer run: lower capital accumulation may reduce potential output growth.

5. Debt Servicing and Financial Pressure

Higher interest rates can also place pressure on households, firms, and governments that already have debt.

However, an important distinction is needed.

Existing fixed-rate debt does not suddenly become more expensive simply because the Federal Reserve raises interest rates.

The immediate burden falls mainly on:

  • Floating-rate loans
  • Adjustable-rate mortgages
  • Credit-card balances
  • Firms with variable-rate debt
  • Borrowers refinancing maturing debt
  • New borrowers

For these borrowers:

\[ i\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Debt-service payments}\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Disposable cash flow}\downarrow. \]

Households may therefore reduce consumption, while firms may reduce hiring or investment.

This is one reason monetary policy often works with a lag. Existing low-interest debt gradually matures and is refinanced at higher rates.

6. Net Exports

The exchange-rate effect feeds back into the real economy.

If the U.S. dollar appreciates:

  • U.S. exports become more expensive to foreigners.
  • Imported goods become cheaper for U.S. consumers.

Therefore:

\[ \$\uparrow \Rightarrow X\downarrow,\ M\uparrow \Rightarrow NX\downarrow. \]

Since net exports are part of aggregate demand, this provides another channel through which higher interest rates can cool the economy.

7. Asset Prices and Wealth

Higher interest rates can also reduce the present value of future income from financial and real assets.

This may place downward pressure on:

  • Bond prices
  • Equity prices
  • Property prices

Lower asset values can reduce household wealth and may also reduce the value of collateral available for borrowing.

This creates another possible transmission mechanism:

\[ i\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Asset prices}\downarrow \Rightarrow \text{Wealth and collateral}\downarrow \Rightarrow C,\ I\downarrow. \]

8. International Spillovers

A U.S. interest-rate increase does not affect only the United States.

Higher U.S. yields may attract international capital toward dollar-denominated assets.

For other countries this can mean:

  • Capital outflows
  • Depreciation pressure on their currencies
  • Higher domestic interest rates if central banks respond
  • More expensive servicing of U.S.-dollar-denominated debt

These effects can be especially important for emerging economies with substantial dollar debt.

9. Putting the Channels Together

The main effects can be summarized as follows:

\[ \boxed{\text{Fed raises interest rates}} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \begin{array}{ccccc} \text{Borrowing cost}\uparrow & \text{Bond prices}\downarrow & \text{Dollar}\uparrow & \text{Asset prices}\downarrow & \text{Debt service}\uparrow \end{array} \]

which contributes to:

\[ C\downarrow,\qquad I\downarrow,\qquad NX\downarrow \]

and therefore:

\[ \boxed{ AD\downarrow \Rightarrow \text{Economic activity cools} \Rightarrow \text{Inflationary pressure falls} } \]

The key point is that monetary policy does not operate through a single channel.

A change in the Federal Reserve’s policy rate affects borrowing, saving, asset prices, exchange rates, investment, consumption, expectations, and international capital flows.

These effects also do not occur immediately. Monetary policy typically works through the economy gradually, and different channels operate at different speeds.

Raising interest rates is therefore best understood as an attempt to reduce current demand and inflationary pressure by making present spending more expensive relative to future spending.

10. A Final Qualification

An interest-rate increase does not necessarily mean that GDP must immediately fall or that the economy must enter recession.

If the economy is growing strongly, tighter monetary policy may simply slow the rate of growth:

\[ \text{Growth without tightening} > \text{Growth with tightening} > 0. \]

The objective of monetary tightening is often precisely this: to cool an overheating economy sufficiently to reduce inflation without producing an unnecessarily severe contraction.

Whether the Federal Reserve succeeds depends on how strongly households, firms, financial markets, and inflation expectations respond.

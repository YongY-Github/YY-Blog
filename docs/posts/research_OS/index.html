Research OS: Externalizing Academic Thinking in the Age of AI

For many years, I worked in what I now realize was a highly fragmented academic workflow.

Like many academics, I had:

  • papers in multiple folders,
  • half-finished drafts,
  • disconnected notes,
  • ideas scattered across notebooks and chats,
  • and many projects simultaneously competing for attention.

The problem was not lack of ideas.

If anything, the opposite was true.

The real problem was that intellectual life had become too cognitively fragmented to manage purely “in the head.”

Recently, however, I began experimenting with a lightweight system that I now think of as a:

Research OS (Research Operating System)

What surprised me is that the effect was immediate and psychologically noticeable.

Projects that previously felt:

  • overwhelming,
  • diffuse,
  • or difficult to restart

suddenly began to feel:

  • resumable,
  • operational,
  • and cognitively manageable.

This blog post is an attempt to clarify what this system is, how it relates to existing ideas like PKM and “Second Brain” systems, and why I suspect it may become increasingly important in the age of AI.

The Core Problem: Cognitive Reload Cost

One of the hidden costs of intellectual work is what I call:

cognitive reload cost

Suppose you stop working on a paper or book chapter for several days.

Restarting the project often requires:

  • remembering where files are,
  • reconstructing previous decisions,
  • recalling unresolved issues,
  • rediscovering bottlenecks,
  • and mentally re-entering the project.

This process is surprisingly exhausting.

In fact, many projects stall not because the work itself is impossible, but because:

  • the activation energy becomes too high,
  • and the intellectual state evaporates between work sessions.

This becomes especially severe when working across:

  • multiple papers,
  • teaching,
  • administration,
  • coding,
  • AI tools,
  • and long-horizon book projects.

The traditional academic workflow was never really designed for this level of fragmentation.

What Is a Research OS?

A Research OS is not simply:

  • note-taking,
  • task management,
  • or file organization.

Rather, it is a lightweight system for:

  • preserving intellectual continuity,
  • reducing restart friction,
  • externalizing working memory,
  • and maintaining operational awareness across projects.

My current system is intentionally simple.

For each major project, I maintain a small collection of markdown files:

brain.md
daily_log.md
ideas.md
todo.md
format.md

The exact filenames do not matter.

What matters is the function.

The Key Components

1. brain.md

This is the strategic control room.

It contains:

  • project philosophy,
  • current focus,
  • bottlenecks,
  • operational rules,
  • weekly targets,
  • and next actions.

The key idea is:

the file reflects current operational reality.

Not aspirations. Not archival history.

Example:

# Current Focus

1. Finish GT manuscript
2. Applied Econometrics book
3. Volatility paper

# Bottlenecks

- Volatility paper: re-estimation
- Applied Econometrics: momentum

# Next Actions

- Create Chapter 3 markdown file
- Rerun GCC-GARCH estimation

This dramatically reduces ambiguity.

2. daily_log.md

This is not journaling.

It is a continuity log.

Example:

## 2026-05-12

- Completed first GT review pass
- Paused GT polish until weekend
- Shifted focus to grading and Japan paperwork

Over time, this becomes:

  • a memory extension,
  • a progress archive,
  • and a restart mechanism.

3. ideas.md

This is extremely important for creative thinkers.

The purpose is:

capture ideas without allowing them to derail active work.

Examples:

  • possible examples,
  • future papers,
  • blog ideas,
  • diagrams,
  • pedagogical thoughts,
  • philosophical reflections.

Without this file, the brain keeps rehearsing:

“Don’t forget this idea.”

With it, the idea is safely parked.

4. format.md

This file preserves stylistic and pedagogical consistency.

For example:

  • chapter structure,
  • notation conventions,
  • box types,
  • coding style,
  • exercise format,
  • tone.

This becomes increasingly valuable in long writing projects.

This Is Not Exactly a “Second Brain”

The system overlaps with several existing traditions, but is not identical to any of them.

Personal Knowledge Management (PKM)

PKM systems aim to externalize information and knowledge.

These systems became increasingly popular with digital note-taking tools such as:

  • Notion,
  • Obsidian,
  • Roam,
  • Logseq,
  • and Evernote.

However, many PKM systems gradually become:

  • passive storage systems,
  • collections of links,
  • or elaborate organizational structures rarely revisited.

I experienced exactly this with Notion: I ended up storing many links that I never returned to.

The problem is that storage alone does not solve intellectual workflow problems.

“Second Brain” Systems

The idea of a “Second Brain” (popularized by Tiago Forte) emphasizes:

  • externalized memory,
  • searchable information,
  • and reusable knowledge assets.

This overlaps substantially with a Research OS.

However, many implementations focus heavily on:

  • information capture,
  • categorization,
  • and archiving.

My own system increasingly emphasizes something different:

operational continuity.

The key question is not:

“Can I store this?”

but:

“Can I quickly re-enter and continue meaningful intellectual work?”

Zettelkasten

The Zettelkasten system, associated with sociologist Niklas Luhmann, is based on:

  • atomic notes,
  • dense linking,
  • and emergent knowledge structures.

It is intellectually fascinating and historically influential.

However, my current system is less:

  • idea-network oriented,
  • and more:
  • project-oriented and operational.

I am less concerned with building a giant web of notes and more concerned with:

  • maintaining momentum,
  • reducing restart friction,
  • and finishing large intellectual projects.

Digital Gardens

Digital gardens are more exploratory and public-facing.

They emphasize:

  • evolving thought,
  • unfinished ideas,
  • and nonlinear exploration.

My blog may partially evolve into this.

But again, the Research OS is more operational than expressive.

Why AI Changes Everything

Before AI, notes were mostly passive.

Now they are machine-readable context.

This is a profound shift.

Markdown files can now function as:

  • continuity layers,
  • contextual memory,
  • project state,
  • and collaborative context for AI systems.

For example:

  • brain.md helps restore project philosophy,
  • daily_log.md restores continuity,
  • format.md preserves stylistic consistency.

AI can then:

  • summarize,
  • continue,
  • critique,
  • reorganize,
  • or help resume work.

In other words:

notes are no longer merely archival. They are now interactive cognitive infrastructure.

This is historically new.

The Most Important Concept: Externalized Working Memory

I increasingly think this is the core idea behind the entire system.

Human working memory is limited.

Complex intellectual work fails not because:

  • we lack intelligence,
  • or ideas,
  • but because:
  • cognitive state dissipates over time.

A Research OS externalizes part of this cognitive state.

This reduces:

  • activation energy,
  • fragmentation,
  • and context-switching costs.

The result is psychologically significant.

Projects begin to feel:

resumable rather than overwhelming.

Why This Matters for Students

I suspect this may become increasingly important for students as well.

Modern students face:

  • information overload,
  • fragmented workflows,
  • AI tool chaos,
  • and shallow organizational habits.

Teaching students isolated AI tools may quickly become obsolete.

Teaching students how to build:

  • continuity,
  • workflow systems,
  • externalized cognitive structures,
  • and AI-integrated research habits

may ultimately be far more valuable.

This is one reason why I have started experimenting with the idea of a:

“Research OS” in my Research with AI course.

Final Thoughts

What is interesting is that I did not arrive at this system through productivity culture or optimization trends.

Rather, it emerged because:

  • my intellectual interests expanded,
  • projects multiplied,
  • and the traditional “keep it all in your head” model stopped scaling.

The system is still evolving.

But already, the effects are noticeable:

  • reduced mental clutter,
  • lower restart friction,
  • improved continuity,
  • and clearer operational focus.

Most importantly, it makes long intellectual projects feel alive again.
